How to Cut a Cigar: Every Cut Type Explained

By Stick Picks · Published July 4, 2026

Quick Answer

Cut a cigar just above the shoulder of the cap -- roughly 1/16" to 1/8" from the tip -- using a sharp double-blade guillotine, V-cutter, or punch. One swift, decisive motion prevents wrapper damage. The right cut opens airflow without unraveling the leaf.

The moment before you light a well-aged stick — cap in the cutter, pressure building — shouldn’t involve guesswork. A bad cut can tighten the draw, shred the wrapper, or turn a 90-point cigar into a frustrating chew. This guide covers every cut type, the correct depth, which tool fits which cigar shape, and the mistakes that cost collectors good tobacco.

Why does the cut matter so much — isn’t it just snipping the end off?

A question we hear often: The cut determines airflow, smoke temperature, and how flavor reaches your palate. A wide opening delivers cooler, more voluminous smoke and a fuller taste. A narrower opening concentrates heat and pushes more smoke oil onto a smaller surface, intensifying certain notes — particularly spice and sweetness. Cut too deep and the wrapper unravels mid-smoke; cut too shallow and you’re fighting a plugged draw for the next hour.

Every premium hand-rolled cigar has a cap — a small disk of wrapper leaf applied to the head (the end you put in your mouth) to keep the filler and binder together. The shoulder is the gentle curve where that cap meets the straight body of the cigar. Your target is always just above that shoulder line: remove only the cap, nothing below it. That zone is typically about 1/16” to 1/8” (2-3 mm) from the very tip.

One more anatomy note: the foot is the open end you light. You never cut the foot — it’s already open.

What are the three main cuts, and which one should I use?

There are three cuts you’ll encounter in cigar culture, each producing a meaningfully different smoking experience.

The straight cut is the workhorse. A double-blade guillotine applies simultaneous pressure from both sides, which reduces the chance of the wrapper tearing compared to a single-blade design. It’s the cut Cigar Aficionado and most experienced tobacconists recommend as a starting point because it’s forgiving across all vitola shapes.

The V-cut carves a wedge-shaped channel rather than removing the cap entirely. Because the smoke travels through a narrower path, some flavor notes — particularly pepper and sweetness — read more intensely on the palate. The tradeoff: if a cigar already runs hot or has a generous ring gauge, a V-cut can make that worse. It also doesn’t play well with tapered heads.

The punch cut uses a circular blade to core a small hole in the cap without removing it. The upside is portability — many punch cutters attach to a keychain — and a very clean result when used correctly. The downside is oil and tar buildup at the cap if you smoke too quickly, and it simply won’t seat properly on any pointy or tapered head.

How do I actually make the cut without wrecking the wrapper?

This one comes up a lot: Technique matters as much as tool selection. Here’s the sequence that works for any cut:

  1. Inspect the cap. Roll the cigar slowly under good light and locate the shoulder — that subtle seam where the rounded cap blends into the straight cylinder. This is your landmark, not a measured distance from the tip.
  2. Check humidity. A properly humidified cigar — stored at 65-72% RH — has a supple wrapper that yields cleanly under a blade. A dry cigar has a brittle wrapper that splinters. If your cigars haven’t been resting in your humidor long enough after a delivery, wait before cutting.
  3. Position before you commit. Slide the cigar into your cutter slowly. Before closing the blade, confirm the cutter is aligned perpendicular to the cigar — angled cuts cause uneven burns and structural stress on the cap edge.
  4. Cut in a single, decisive motion. Hesitation is the enemy. Slow, grinding pressure crushes tobacco fibers and tears the wrapper instead of slicing it. One swift close of the blade produces the clean edge you want. Think of it the way Cigar Aficionado puts it: “a true aficionado cuts like a surgeon — quickly and confidently.”
  5. Take a cold draw. Before lighting, pull air through the unlit cigar. A good cut produces a draw that’s open but has some gentle resistance — like breathing through a coffee stirrer. If it’s too tight, trim a hair more (but stop at the shoulder). If it’s completely unrestricted, the cut was too deep; proceed carefully and smoke slowly to avoid overheating.

For torpedoes, belicosos, and other figurados, the tapered head means there’s no single shoulder line to find. Cut incrementally — start conservatively near the tip and test the draw after each small trim until you reach a comfortable resistance. Scissors are particularly well-suited here because they let you guide the cut plane precisely.

Straight cut vs. V-cut: which actually tastes better?

Readers frequently ask: There’s no universal answer — and that’s a feature, not a bug. The same cigar can deliver noticeably different experiences depending on which cut you choose, which makes it worth experimenting deliberately rather than defaulting to one style forever.

A straight cut exposes a large surface area of the filler, delivering what many describe as a fuller, rounder expression of the blend — all flavor components present and roughly equal. It’s the standard in most lounges and the default choice of experienced collectors who want to taste the cigar as the blender intended.

A V-cut narrows the channel, compressing the smoke slightly before it reaches your palate. Anecdotally, this tends to bring forward the middle and back-palate notes — spice, dried fruit, dark chocolate — while softening the initial blast of pepper many full-strength cigars open with. Some enthusiasts use a V-cut specifically on Nicaraguan puros to tame the first third without sacrificing complexity. Others find it too concentrated and prefer the open architecture of a straight cut for the same cigars. Try both on back-to-back examples of the same vitola and decide for yourself — that kind of deliberate tasting is exactly what a cigar journal is built for. If you’re already logging smokes in Stick Picks, adding a “cut type” note to each session takes about five seconds and starts building a useful personal dataset over time.

What makes a cutter sharp enough — and when do I need to replace it?

A dull blade doesn’t cut: it crushes. The wrapper compresses before it separates, producing a jagged, frayed edge that unravels as the cigar burns down. A sharp double-blade guillotine should produce an audible, clean snick and leave an edge that looks almost machined.

Signs your cutter needs attention:

  • The cut edge looks torn or has small shreds of wrapper
  • You need to apply noticeably more pressure than usual
  • The wrapper around the cut starts peeling within the first few puffs

Quality cutters from brands like Xikar include a manufacturer’s sharpening service, often covered under a lifetime warranty. Entry-level cutters (under $15) rarely sharpen well and are typically better replaced than restored once they dull. If you’re investing in cigars at $15-$30 per stick, a $40-$80 cutter is not an extravagance — it’s insurance.

One tip that applies in cold weather: if your metal cutter has been sitting in a car or bag in cool temperatures, warm it briefly in your hand before cutting. Cold metal can micro-crack a dry or delicate wrapper — particularly fragile leaf like Cameroon — before the blade even closes.

Can I cut a cigar without a proper cutter at all?

Yes, though results vary. In descending order of reliability:

  • Sharp pocket knife or folding blade — works well if the blade is genuinely sharp and you can apply consistent, rolling pressure. Draw the blade across the cap rather than pressing straight down.
  • Nail scissors — underrated. Compact, sharp, and precise enough for figurados. Many experienced smokers keep a small pair in their travel kit.
  • Thumbnail — workable on softer caps. Press the nail firmly around the circumference of the cap just above the shoulder and rotate to score before pulling. Leaves a rough edge but keeps the cigar intact.
  • Biting — last resort only. Teeth tear rather than slice, leaving a ragged opening and fragments in your mouth. Better than no cigar, but only just.

Never use a dull kitchen knife, scissors with a flat grind, or anything that requires you to saw back and forth — the lateral motion shreds the wrapper.

Does the humidity level in my humidor actually affect how the cut goes?

A question we hear often: Directly, yes. Wrapper leaf at the correct humidity — 65-72% RH — has a pliable, almost leather-like quality that holds together cleanly under a blade. A cigar that has dried out below 60% RH has a brittle wrapper that can crack or fragment at the moment of the cut, even with a sharp cutter. Conversely, an over-humidified cigar above 75% RH has a soft, spongy cap that compresses rather than slices cleanly, often producing a mushy edge that unravels.

This is one of the practical reasons consistent humidor management pays off in ways you feel during every smoke, not just during long-term aging. If you’ve recently rehydrated a dried cigar, give it at least a few days at proper RH before cutting — the wrapper needs time to re-acclimate fully, not just absorb surface moisture. Our guide to how to rehydrate dry cigars covers the timeline in detail.

The same logic applies to cigars you’ve just received in a shipment. Online retailers often ship in climate-controlled packaging, but transit stress still occurs. Resting new arrivals in your humidor for at least a week before cutting — a topic covered in our article on how long to rest cigars in a humidor after buying — consistently produces cleaner cuts and better first-third performance.

A note on tracking your cuts: If you use Stick Picks to journal your smokes, noting the cut type alongside tasting notes is a simple habit that pays dividends. After a dozen entries, patterns emerge — you may find that your favorite Honduran lanceros always perform better with a V-cut, or that a particular blend’s draw is inconsistent with a punch. That kind of personal data, built over time, is more useful than any generic recommendation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep should you cut a cigar?

Cut only the cap -- about 1/16" to 1/8" (2-3 mm) from the tip. Find the shoulder, the curved seam where the cap meets the cigar body, and cut just above it. Going deeper risks unraveling the wrapper and ruining the draw.

Which cigar cut is best for beginners?

A double-blade guillotine (straight cut) is the best starting point. It works on virtually every cigar shape and ring gauge, delivers a wide, forgiving draw, and is the hardest cut to botch if your cutter is sharp.

Can you cut a cigar without a cutter?

Yes, in a pinch. A sharp pocket knife, nail scissors, or even a thumbnail pressed firmly around the cap can work. The goal is the same: remove only the cap cleanly. Biting the cap off is a last resort -- it tears rather than slices, often fraying the wrapper.

Does the cut affect cigar flavor?

Significantly. A wider straight cut delivers a cooler, fuller smoke. A V-cut concentrates smoke through a narrower channel, intensifying spice and sweetness. A punch cut produces the tightest draw, which can cause tar and oil buildup if you smoke too fast.

Can you cut a torpedo or figurado with a punch cutter?

No -- punch cutters require a flat, round cap to seat properly. Tapered vitolas like torpedoes, belicosos, and perfectos need a shallow straight cut (guillotine or scissors). Attempting a punch on a pointed head usually results in a clogged, uneven draw.

Why is my cigar wrapper unraveling after cutting?

You've cut past the shoulder into the body of the cigar, or the cigar is too dry. Wrapper leaves grip the cap tightly only up to the shoulder seam. Always verify your cut point before snipping, and make sure your cigars are properly humidified -- a dry wrapper cracks and separates far more easily under a blade.
