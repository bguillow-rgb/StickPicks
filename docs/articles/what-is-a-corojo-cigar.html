What Is a Corojo Cigar? Flavor, History & What to Buy

By Stick Picks · Published October 5, 2026

Quick Answer

A Corojo cigar uses a Cuban-seed wrapper leaf originally grown at Cuba's El Corojo farm in the 1930s. Known for red pepper, sweet cedar, and caramel notes in a medium-to-full body, true Corojo is now grown mainly in Honduras and Nicaragua, and it remains one of the most prized wrappers in premium cigar collecting.

Spend any time browsing cigar bands or talking shop at your local lounge and you’ll hear “Corojo” come up with a reverence usually reserved for a great vintage wine. Unless you already know the backstory, though, the word can feel like insider jargon, one more term to decode before you can confidently shop your humidor. Here’s everything you need to know, from the tobacco’s Cuban roots to why the regional address on your band matters more than the name alone.

What exactly is a Corojo cigar, and where does the name come from?

Corojo (pronounced “ko-RO-ho”) is a sun-grown Cuban-seed tobacco developed in the 1930s at the famed El Corojo farm in Pinar del Río. The name stuck to the leaf itself, not just the farm. It earned a reputation for rich aroma and pronounced pepper spice, and became Cuba’s standard wrapper until the 1990s, when blue-mold outbreaks forced growers to switch to hardier hybrids.

Corojo served as the wrapper for Cuban cigars across the board for decades, with its spicy character becoming a defining trait of the country’s tobacco. Between the 1930s and the 1990s, all Cuban cigars, regardless of brand or factory, used Corojo wrappers. That’s roughly six decades of near-total market dominance, which goes a long way toward explaining why the name still carries weight.

Today the story is different. Pure Corojo tobacco is no longer grown in Cuba. Corojo wrappers are now harvested in the Jamastrán region of Honduras, and additional Corojo varietals come from hybrid seeds in other Central American countries, including Nicaragua.

What does a Corojo cigar actually taste like?

A question we hear often: collectors upgrading from Connecticut Shade or mild maduros want to know what they’re getting into before committing to a box.

Corojo delivers a medium-plus body with red pepper, sweet cedar, caramel, and hints of citrus zest. The wrapper’s oil content produces an aromatic room note and a sturdy, off-white ash. The pepper hit is usually up front. You’ll notice it on the first third, and it tends to mellow into the sweeter cedar and caramel notes as the cigar burns down.

The leaves often cure to a darker brown with an oily, sometimes toothy surface. They’re also naturally sweet and often creamy in the finish, which balances the spice and keeps the cigar from feeling aggressive. Body sits solidly in the medium-to-full range, making Corojo a reasonable bridge between a mild everyday smoke and a full-on powerhouse Nicaraguan puro.

How does Honduran Corojo differ from Nicaraguan Corojo?

This one comes up a lot: the same seed variety grown in different soil produces noticeably different results, and collectors who’ve tried both describe the gap as significant.

Most Corojo cigars feature notable pepper, but sweetness and body vary by region and blend. Honduran Corojo is generally spicier. The Jamastrán Valley’s rich volcanic soil amplifies the leaf’s natural earthiness and ramps up the pepper intensity. Nicaraguan Corojo, particularly from the Jalapa Valley, tends to run a touch smoother, with more sweetness and slightly more complexity in the middle third.

A Corojo from Jamastrán isn’t the same smoke as one grown in Jalapa. Terroir is as real a concept in tobacco as it is in wine: same seed, different mineral profile in the soil, meaningfully different result. When you’re shopping for Corojo cigars, treat the growing region as a filter just like you would a wrapper color or ring gauge.

What’s the difference between original Corojo and Corojo ‘99?

Modern hybrids, Corojo ‘99 and Corojo ‘06, were bred for blue-mold resistance while keeping the classic flavor. Corojo ‘99 is the most widely planted of these and the one you’ll encounter most often on band descriptions. The original leaf’s high susceptibility to black molds and black shank disease is what pushed growers toward hybrid development in the first place.

From a flavor standpoint, the hybrid performs remarkably close to the original. The pepper, cedar, and caramel signature is largely intact. The trade-off is a small reduction in the variation and complexity that old-school purists associate with pre-hybrid Corojo. Corojo ‘99 is planted in Ecuador, Western Kentucky, and Nicaragua, and the Nicaraguan version, an Ecuador transplant of the original ‘99, brings considerable complexity of its own. If you’re building a cellar and see “Corojo ‘99” on the box, treat it as a confident modern expression of the classic, not a cut-rate substitute.

Are Corojo cigars good for collectors, or just for daily smoking?

Readers frequently ask: whether Corojo is worth laying down or if it’s best smoked young.

Corojo’s flavor profile makes it a solid aging candidate, though it doesn’t follow the same long-arc development as a thicker, denser Nicaraguan puro loaded with ligero filler. The pepper tends to soften and integrate over 2-4 years of proper humidor storage, while the cedar and caramel notes deepen. Most experienced collectors find that 18 months to 3 years of rest is the sweet spot for Corojo, long enough to round out the spice, short enough that you don’t lose the vitality that makes the wrapper worth buying in the first place.

Aging is an important process in cigar production, occurring after rolling and further reducing irritation while improving the aftertaste of a cigar. The same principle applies to aging in your personal humidor. The chemical components of cigars tend to harmonize over time, and sensory quality can improve further with age. In general, cigar aging should occur in a stable environment, around 20°C (plus or minus 2°C) and 62% relative humidity (plus or minus 2%). For most Corojo lines, a humidor dialed to 65-68% RH and 68-70°F is plenty.

What are the best Corojo cigars to look for?

A question we hear often from collectors stepping into the segment for the first time.

The clearest way to find the genuine article is to select cigars with “Corojo” listed as their wrapper, though Habano 2000 and Criollo ‘98 are both hybrids of Corojo if you want adjacent options. These cigars come from a range of boutique manufacturers and represent the best of their modern-day expressions. A few anchors worth knowing:

  • Camacho Corojo: The cigar that sparked the modern Corojo revival. Christian Eiroa of C.L.E. Cigars resurrected Corojo just ahead of the cigar boom with the Camacho brand. Made entirely of Corojo leaf from Jamastrán, it remains the benchmark against which other Honduran Corojos are judged.
  • C.L.E. Corojo: Eiroa’s follow-up, with a distinct personality. Notes of salt, earthiness, brown sugar, cedar, and spice make it its own animal.
  • Punch Rare Corojo: A long-running Honduran benchmark in the segment, worth seeking out for a pure Jamastrán expression.
  • Nicaraguan Corojo ‘99 blends: A wide range of boutique cigars use this hybrid. Look for anything from the Jalapa region for the smoothest entry point.

Habano 2000, a hybrid cross between Corojo and milder Cuban tobacco, is grown in Nicaragua, Ecuador, and Honduras. If you see “Habano 2000” listed on a band, you’re getting a Corojo-adjacent smoke that’s typically a notch milder on the pepper scale.

FAQs

What does a Corojo cigar taste like? Corojo cigars typically deliver red pepper spice, sweet cedar, caramel, and occasional citrus zest in a medium to medium-full body. Honduran Corojo tends to be spicier and earthier; Nicaraguan Corojo is often smoother with more sweetness. The oily wrapper produces a rich room note and a firm, off-white ash.

Is Corojo the same as Habano? Not exactly. Corojo is a specific Cuban-seed tobacco variety used almost exclusively as a wrapper leaf. Habano is a broader term that can refer to any Cuban-seed tobacco, including hybrids like Habano 2000, which is itself a cross between Corojo and milder Cuban strains. All pure Corojo is Cuban-seed, but not all Cuban-seed wrappers are Corojo.

Does Cuba still grow Corojo tobacco? No. Corojo seed was first developed in the 1930s at the El Corojo plantation outside San Juan y Martinez, Cuba, where the leaf was used to wrap Cuba’s finest cigars. Descendants of the seed are now grown in Central America and the Caribbean, but Cuba no longer grows Corojo.

What is the difference between Corojo ‘99 and original Corojo? Corojo ‘99 is a hybrid bred from original Corojo seed for resistance to blue mold and black shank disease while preserving the classic flavor profile. It retains the peppery spice and sweet cedar notes but tends to be slightly more consistent from leaf to leaf. Original Corojo was more delicate and susceptible to disease but is considered the gold standard by purists.

Are Corojo cigars good for beginners? Mild-to-medium smokers should approach Corojo carefully. Most Corojo cigars land in the medium-full strength range, which can be intense for those used to Connecticut Shade. A good entry point is a Nicaraguan Corojo in a shorter format like a robusto, which keeps the smoking time manageable and the nicotine load lower.
