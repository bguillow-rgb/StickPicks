The 135 Rule for Cigars: Humidor Math Made Simple

By Stick Picks · Published November 19, 2026

Quick Answer

The 135 rule for cigars states that your humidor's temperature in Fahrenheit plus its relative humidity percentage should total approximately 135. For example: 70°F + 65% RH = 135. It's a simple way to balance both variables together and keep cigars in the ideal storage zone.

The cigar hobby is full of rules of thumb, some useful, many overstated. The 135 rule falls in the useful category. It’s a quick sanity check that tells you whether your humidor’s temperature and humidity are working together rather than against each other. If you’ve seen it referenced on humidor review sites or in Reddit threads and wondered what it actually means, here’s the full answer.

What exactly is the 135 rule, and where did it come from?

The 135 rule states that your humidor’s temperature in Fahrenheit, added to its relative humidity percentage, should equal 135. If your temperature is 70°F, your humidity should be 65%.

The formula is simply:

Temperature (°F) + Relative Humidity (%) = ~135

Nobody published a white paper on it. The rule emerged from decades of collector experience shared across cigar forums, B&M shop conversations, and industry blogs.

It’s best understood as an improvement on the older 70/70 rule, which sets a single fixed target of 70°F and 70% RH. The 135 rule lets you shift humidity up or down as temperature changes, so you’re managing the relationship between the two variables rather than chasing a static number.

That flexibility is the whole point. Your storage room doesn’t stay at 70°F year-round, so a rule that moves with the temperature is more practical than one that doesn’t.

Why does balancing temperature AND humidity matter, can’t I just track RH alone?

A question we hear often: collectors upgrading from a beginner setup frequently ask why both readings matter when most humidors only display humidity.

Temperature and humidity are not independent variables inside a humidor. They interact constantly. Warmer air holds more moisture, which means the same RH reading at 75°F represents considerably more water vapor pressing against your wrapper than the same reading at 65°F.

The reason this matters most is tobacco beetles. They’re naturally present in tobacco leaves and they wake up fast when things get too warm and too humid at the same time. Once they hatch, they tunnel straight through your cigars and there’s no recovering from that.

Running too hot and too humid together, even when neither number looks alarming on its own, creates a compounding problem. The moisture inside the tobacco expands beyond what the wrapper can handle. You end up with cigars that are soft, spongy, and nearly impossible to draw from.

The 135 rule collapses two readings into one number so you can spot trouble at a glance. A score of 133 or 134? Fine. A score of 139 in mid-July because your climate control forgot your study gets afternoon sun? Time to act. Tracking both metrics in Stick Picks’ humidor log means you always have a time-stamped record when something shifts.

What are the most common 135-rule settings collectors actually use?

Common combinations that satisfy the rule: 65°F + 70% RH = 135, 68°F + 67% RH = 135, 70°F + 65% RH = 135. The guideline covers a range of real-world conditions while keeping cigars in the sweet spot, generally 63, 72% RH and under 75°F.

How is the 135 rule different from the old 70/70 rule?

This one comes up a lot, especially when newer collectors run into the 135 rule on a humidor review and can’t figure out how it relates to what they’ve always heard.

The 70/70 rule is simpler: store cigars at 70°F and 70% relative humidity. Easy to remember, and it was the dominant advice for decades. The problem is that it treats temperature and humidity as fixed targets rather than a relationship, and in the real world, temperature changes constantly with the seasons, the HVAC system, and the room your humidor sits in.

Many experienced collectors actually land around 68, 70°F and 65, 68% humidity. The classic 70/70 puts you right at the edge, especially in summer. At 70°F and 70% RH, your score is 140, which is meaningfully above the 135 target. That’s not a catastrophe, cigars have survived at 70/70 for generations, but it leaves very little margin before mold and beetle conditions arrive. The 135 rule builds that margin in by design.

Think of 70/70 as a fixed address and 135 as a flexible neighborhood. The neighborhood gives you room to move.

What happens if my score creeps above 135?

The goal is a storage environment where the tobacco is neither dry enough to burn hot nor damp enough to grow mold. Keeping the sum at 135 means that as temperature rises, humidity comes down to compensate, which protects the structural integrity of the leaf.

When the score climbs well above 135, the risks stack up:

  • Mold growth. Mold spores are present in every humidor at low levels. Combined heat and humidity accelerate their growth fast.
  • Wrapper swelling and splitting. Excess moisture causes the wrapper to expand unevenly, leading to cracks and unraveling, the same problem covered in our guide to why cigar wrappers crack.
  • Draw problems. A waterlogged filler compresses and clogs, producing the tight, frustrating draw many collectors blame on the cigar when storage is actually the culprit.
  • Tobacco beetle hatching. Larvae are dormant below 70°F but activate quickly above it. Warm temperature combined with high humidity is what tips them over.

The 75°F ceiling is firm. The 135 rule is flexible below it, non-negotiable above.

Readers frequently ask: does a score below 135 mean my cigars are too dry?

Not automatically. A score of 130, say 65°F and 65% RH, lands you at the drier end of the ideal range. Cigars stored there won’t be in danger, but they’ll be firmer to the pinch and may smoke slightly faster and hotter than a well-hydrated cigar. For long-term aging, many serious collectors actually prefer operating in the 130, 133 range: cooler and drier conditions slow the aging process and reduce the risk of anything going wrong while cigars rest untouched for years.

The concern kicks in below roughly 60% RH, where wrapper cracking becomes likely and the tobacco begins losing the volatile aromatic compounds that make a well-aged cigar interesting. Use our guide on how to tell if a cigar is too dry if you’re not sure your sticks are in good shape.

How do I actually apply the 135 rule to my humidor setup?

Applying it takes about a minute once you have a reliable digital hygrometer/thermometer combo inside your humidor, always a worthwhile investment over an analog unit, as discussed in our digital vs analog hygrometer guide.

Step 1: Read your current temperature and humidity from the display. Step 2: Add them. Temperature + RH = your score. Step 3: Compare to 135.

  • Score 132, 137: Acceptable. No action needed.
  • Score 138, 142: Borderline. Lower humidity by 2, 3 points by dry-boxing a few sticks or cracking the humidor briefly to vent.
  • Score 143+: Act now. Remove cigars temporarily, identify the heat source, and adjust your humidification solution downward.
  • Score below 128: Check for a drying humidifier element or a lid seal failure.

Instead of treating temperature and humidity as two separate concerns, the 135 rule gives you one number to check. Logging that score alongside your humidity readings in a tracker like Stick Picks gives you a running history, so you can see whether last August’s heatwave quietly pushed your score into dangerous territory.

FAQs: the 135 rule for cigars

What is the 135 rule for cigars? The 135 rule states that your humidor’s temperature (in °F) plus its relative humidity percentage should equal roughly 135. If your humidor is at 70°F, target 65% RH. If it runs cooler at 65°F, you can safely allow up to 70% RH.

What’s the difference between the 70/70 rule and the 135 rule? The older 70/70 rule simply says store cigars at 70°F and 70% RH, a single fixed target. The 135 rule is more flexible: it lets you adjust humidity up or down as your room temperature changes, keeping the two variables balanced rather than chasing one static number.

What happens if my 135 rule score goes above 135? A score meaningfully above 135 means your cigars are exposed to a combination of heat and humidity that risks mold, wrapper swelling, draw problems, and above 75°F, the hatching of tobacco beetle larvae. Bring your temperature or humidity down promptly.

Does the 135 rule work in Celsius? No, the formula only works with Fahrenheit. If your thermometer reads in Celsius, convert first: °C x 9/5 + 32 = °F. At 21°C (70°F) and 65% RH, your score correctly equals 135.

Is 65% RH too low for long-term cigar aging? Not at all. Many experienced collectors prefer 65, 68% RH for long-term aging because drier conditions allow slower, more even development without the mold risk that comes at higher humidity. Stability matters more than hitting a specific number.

How often should I check my 135 rule score? Check temperature and humidity whenever seasons change, typically every few weeks in summer and winter, when ambient room temperature shifts most dramatically. A digital hygrometer/thermometer combo makes this a 10-second habit.
