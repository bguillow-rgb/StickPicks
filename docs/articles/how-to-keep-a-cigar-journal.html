How to Keep a Cigar Journal: A Collector's Guide

By Stick Picks · Published June 25, 2026

Quick Answer

Keep a cigar journal by recording the brand, vitola, wrapper type, date smoked, and tasting notes for each cigar, divided into thirds (first, middle, final third). A consistent log trains your palate, guides future purchases, and tracks how your collection ages over time.

There’s a particular kind of collector frustration that doesn’t get talked about enough: you smoke something extraordinary six months ago, and now you can’t remember whether it was a Robusto or a Toro, Nicaraguan or Dominican, or whether the retailer even still carries it. A cigar journal solves that problem, and does a lot more besides.

Why would I bother keeping notes on cigars I’ve already smoked?

A question we hear often: Most collectors discover the answer only after they’ve lost it — the memory of a standout smoke that slipped away because nothing was written down.

A journal does three concrete things. First, it builds a personalized reference library that no magazine rating can replicate, because it’s calibrated to your palate, not a panel’s consensus. Second, it accelerates palate development: when you write down what you taste, you notice more with every cigar you light. Third, it turns your humidor from a static storage box into a living record — you can trace how the same blend evolves across months or years of aging.

Think of it the way a wine enthusiast uses a cellar book. Opening a Barolo and recording its profile, then doing it again three years later, is what turns a collection into a longitudinal study of your own taste. Cigar collecting works the same way. Industry educators note that collectors who journal consistently report making better purchase decisions within their first year, simply because they stop repeating blends they didn’t enjoy and double down on the ones that resonated.

What exactly should I write down for each cigar?

This one comes up a lot. The good news: a complete entry doesn’t require much time. Here are the essential fields:

FieldWhy It Matters
Brand & lineIdentifies the cigar precisely (e.g., Oliva Serie V, not just “Oliva”)
VitolaSize and ring gauge — affects burn time and flavor concentration
Wrapper originOften the single biggest flavor driver
Date purchasedLets you calculate rest/age time
Date smokedThe anchor for all aging comparisons
Price per stickHelps evaluate value over time
Construction notesDraw resistance, burn evenness, ash quality
Flavor notes by thirdFirst, middle, and final third evolve differently
PairingWhat you drank alongside it
Rating (1-10)Your bottom-line verdict
Would buy again?A simple yes/no that’s surprisingly useful at purchase time

You don’t need every field every time. Even a stripped-down entry — brand, wrapper, date, two sentences of flavor notes, and a rating — beats nothing by a wide margin. The goal is consistency, not completeness.

How do I actually describe what a cigar tastes like?

Readers frequently ask: “I know I taste something, but I don’t have the vocabulary.”

Start with a cigar flavor wheel, which clusters notes into recognizable families: wood (cedar, oak, hickory), earth (leather, soil, barnyard), spice (pepper, cinnamon, clove), cream (butter, milk chocolate, nougat), and fruit (dried cherry, raisin, citrus zest). You don’t need to nail a specific note. Writing “dry cedar with a hint of dark chocolate” is entirely valid and infinitely more useful to future-you than a blank page.

The three-thirds structure is your best framework. Most cigars behave differently across their length because oils and flavors concentrate as the tobacco burns down:

  • First third: The cold draw aroma before lighting, then the first flavors after the initial puffs settle. Often the mildest and most aromatic phase.
  • Second third (midpoint): Where complexity typically peaks. Strength builds and secondary flavors appear. This is the section most worth documenting carefully.
  • Final third: Strength tends to peak here. Some cigars finish superbly; others turn harsh. Note whether the finish was long or short, clean or bitter.

Don’t strain for sophistication. Experienced reviewers point out that the most common pitfall in cigar notes is using elaborate vocabulary to describe something you don’t actually taste, which makes the journal useless as a future reference. Write what you genuinely notice, even if it’s as simple as “tastes like a campfire and espresso — really liked it.”

Physical notebook, spreadsheet, or app — which format actually works?

All three have real advantages and real drawbacks.

Physical notebook: The tactile experience fits naturally with the ritual of cigar smoking. Many enthusiasts tape or affix the cigar band directly to the page alongside their notes, which helps when re-ordering. The downside is search — finding a specific entry from two years ago means flipping pages.

Spreadsheet: Highly searchable and customizable. You can sort by rating, filter by wrapper type, or calculate your average spend per cigar. The friction of opening a laptop mid-smoke means most people end up writing shorthand on their phone and transferring later, which is a perfectly fine workflow.

Dedicated cigar app: The most practical option for active collectors. Apps like Stick Picks let you log a smoke directly from your humidor catalog — so the cigar is already identified, the purchase date is already there, and your journal entry links directly to the stick’s record. Scanning a band to populate the brand and blend details automatically saves the most tedious part of any entry.

For collectors who own more than 50 cigars, the searchability of a digital format pays off quickly. Pulling up every Maduro you’ve ever rated above an 8, in seconds, is the kind of insight a paper notebook can’t match without significant indexing effort.

How do I use my journal to actually get better at buying cigars?

This one comes up a lot: collectors build journals but rarely mine them strategically.

Once you have 20 or 30 entries, patterns emerge fast. You might find you consistently rate Cameroon-wrapped cigars higher than Habano despite having assumed the opposite. Or that every cigar you smoked after dinner scored two points higher than identical cigars smoked mid-afternoon — because context matters enormously. A cigar smoked with bourbon on a cool porch almost always outperforms the same stick with water at a desk.

A practical exercise: every three months, sort your entries by rating and look at the top five and bottom five. Check what the top performers have in common — region, wrapper family, body level, price bracket. That cluster is your personal sweet spot, and it should inform the next box you order. The bottom five are equally valuable: they’re your do-not-repeat list, which is real money saved.

There’s also the aging dimension. If you log both a purchase date and a smoke date for every entry, you can eventually compare the same blend smoked at different stages of humidor rest. A cigar that scored a 7 right out of the box might score a 9 after six months of acclimation, or vice versa. Without the dates, that comparison is guesswork.

Should I note the pairing, the setting, and who I was with?

Yes — and this is the part that separates a useful journal from a dry data log. Context profoundly shapes the experience of a cigar. As one long-running industry publication puts it, many enthusiasts have smoked very average cigars in excellent company and remembered them more fondly than expensive sticks smoked alone in poor conditions.

Logging the pairing (bourbon, coffee, mezcal, sparkling water) is especially useful because certain flavor profiles in tobacco interact dramatically with certain beverages. If you find a specific combination that elevates a mid-range cigar, that’s knowledge worth capturing. Note the setting too — back porch, lounge, golf course — because you’ll recall the entry more vividly, and some cigars genuinely suit certain environments.

You don’t need a paragraph for every entry. A single line — “back porch, Tuesday evening, Blanton’s bourbon, wife’s birthday” — is enough to anchor the memory years later.

How many entries should I have before my journal becomes genuinely useful?

The inflection point most collectors notice is around 25-30 entries. At that volume, patterns in your preferences start to crystallize clearly: you’ll begin to see which wrappers, regions, and strength levels consistently earn your top marks. Before that, you’re gathering raw data. After that, you’re analyzing it.

For collectors building their humidor intentionally, Stick Picks’ built-in journal ties each smoke entry directly to the cigar’s humidor record — so you never have to manually re-enter blend details, and every entry accumulates into a searchable history of your collection. The journal and the catalog reinforce each other: what you’ve smoked informs what you choose to stock and age, and your aging notes help you decide when a cigar has rested long enough to smoke at its best.

Start with one entry tonight. The only journal that doesn’t help you is the one that stays blank.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the minimum I should write in a cigar journal entry?

At minimum, log the brand and line, vitola (size and ring gauge), wrapper origin, the date you smoked it, and a one-sentence flavor impression. Adding a 1-10 rating and a 'would buy again?' checkbox rounds out a usable entry in under two minutes.

Should I write tasting notes while I'm smoking or after?

Both, ideally. Jot brief impressions at the first third, midpoint, and final third while you smoke -- flavor evolves significantly across a cigar's length. Then add a summary impression after you've finished. Writing only after the fact tends to blur the nuances you noticed mid-smoke.

Do I need a physical notebook or is a digital journal better?

Neither is universally better -- it comes down to your workflow. Physical notebooks are great at the lounge but hard to search. Digital apps let you filter by wrapper, region, or rating instantly and sync across devices. Many collectors use both: quick notes on paper during the smoke, then transferred to an app later.

How do I describe cigar flavors if I'm not great at tasting notes?

Use a cigar flavor wheel to start -- it clusters common notes into families like wood, earth, spice, cream, and fruit. You don't need professional vocabulary. Writing 'tastes like morning coffee and dry cedar' is more useful to future-you than forcing yourself to name a specific tobacco varietal you can't identify.

Can a cigar journal help me understand how aging changes a cigar?

Absolutely -- it's one of the most valuable uses. When you log the purchase date and smoke date for every entry, comparing notes on the same blend smoked at 3 months vs. 18 months reveals exactly how time in the humidor affects flavor, strength, and smoothness. Without records, that comparison is just a guess.

What should I do with old journal entries?

Mine them for purchase decisions. Sort by your highest-rated cigars to build your core rotation, flag 'would not buy again' entries to avoid repeating mistakes, and look for patterns -- maybe you consistently prefer Nicaraguan fillers or find Connecticut wrappers too mild. Over time your journal becomes a personalized buying guide no critic can replicate.
