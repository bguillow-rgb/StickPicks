Cigar Foot Types: Open, Closed & Shaggy Explained

By Stick Picks · Published August 20, 2026

Quick Answer

A cigar's foot is the end you light. An open foot exposes the filler leaves for a standard light. A closed foot wraps the lighting end in an extra layer of wrapper leaf, requiring a longer toast. A shaggy foot leaves the filler slightly protruding beyond the wrapper for an immediate, intense flavor hit from the first draw.

The foot of a cigar is the end you light, and for most of cigar history it looked the same: open, flat, with a ring of exposed filler and binder waiting for a flame. Then closed-foot and shaggy-foot construction came along, and suddenly a detail most beginners never noticed became one of the most talked-about variables in premium cigar design. If you’ve ever stared at an unusual-looking lighter end and wondered what you’re actually holding, this guide is for you.

Wait — what exactly is the foot of a cigar, and how is it different from the head?

Every cigar has two ends. The head is the closed, rounded end sealed with a small cap of tobacco leaf, the end you cut and put to your lips. The foot is the opposite end, the one you light. On a standard cigar the foot is open, meaning the filler leaves and binder are visible right at the rim of the wrapper. You never cut the foot; you only toast or flame it.

That distinction matters because the three foot variations — open, closed, and shaggy — each behave differently from the moment you pick up your lighter. Get the approach wrong and you’ll either scorch a delicate closed foot or waste time over-toasting an already-eager shaggy foot. For a deeper look at the full anatomy of a cigar from cap to foot, the cigar parts anatomy guide covers every section in detail.

What is a standard open foot cigar, and is it still the most common?

A question we hear often: is the open foot still the default, or has the industry moved toward more elaborate constructions?

The open foot remains by far the most prevalent format in premium handmade cigars. It is exactly what it sounds like: the wrapper terminates cleanly at the lighting end, leaving the filler and binder exposed in a neat, flat or slightly uneven ring. No extra leaf, no loose tobacco overhanging the edge. You toast the foot in a slow, rotating motion, let the ember form evenly across the full face, then draw. The blend’s full character, wrapper, binder, filler in combination, is present from the very first puff.

The open foot rewards proper toasting technique. Rushing the light is one of the most common reasons cigars start with an uneven burn or a bitter, acrid hit. Hold the flame a half-inch below the foot and rotate for a full 20-30 seconds before ever drawing. According to industry educators, the majority of burn problems trace back to inadequate toasting at this stage, not to the cigar itself.

What is a closed foot cigar, and why do blenders use it?

This one comes up a lot: collectors see the wrapped-over end and aren’t sure whether they’ve received a defective cigar or a deliberate design choice.

A closed foot cigar has wrapper-grade tobacco folded or twisted over the lighting end, fully or partially enclosing the filler and binder inside. It looks finished on both ends, almost symmetrical, and often features a small twist or a neatly folded tuck at the tip. No filler is visible.

The closed foot serves two purposes blenders openly talk about. First, it’s visual theater: a closed-foot cigar looks distinctive on a shelf and signals premium construction. Second, and more important to the smoking experience, it front-loads pure wrapper flavor. As you toast the closed end, the wrapper leaf burns back before the filler and binder ignite. For the first few minutes of the smoke you’re tasting wrapper character almost exclusively. On a dark Maduro or a rich Corojo wrapper, that opening note can be extraordinary: concentrated, sweet, or deeply peppery depending on the leaf. Only after the wrapper burns away does the full three-component blend come into play.

Lighting a closed foot requires patience. Use a torch or cedar spill and toast the twisted end slowly in broad, circular passes. Expect the toasting stage to take 45 seconds to a full minute, roughly twice as long as an open foot. Never rush it with direct flame contact; you risk scorching the wrapper edge unevenly and creating a burn problem that follows you through the entire smoke. Some smokers prefer to gently snip the closed end with a straight cutter, treating it as an open foot. That’s perfectly valid, though you sacrifice the wrapper-forward introduction the maker intended.

What is a shaggy foot cigar?

Readers frequently ask: whether the “loose” end of certain cigars is a sign of poor rolling or an intentional feature.

A shaggy foot (sometimes called a “flag” foot) is the opposite of a closed foot: instead of the wrapper covering the lighting end, the filler extends slightly beyond the wrapper’s edge, creating a fringe or brush of loose tobacco strands at the foot. The wrapper terminates a few millimeters short of the filler’s tip, leaving a slightly rough, textured look.

This is 100% intentional. The exposed filler catches flame quickly and easily, and because the tobacco at the foot is filler-dominant, the opening draws are often bolder, more immediate, and more tobacco-forward than you’d get from either an open or closed foot. There’s no wrapper buffer; you’re tasting the blend’s core filler character right away. Many premium Nicaraguan boutique cigars favor shaggy foot construction precisely because their full-bodied fillers have something compelling to say from the very first draw.

When lighting a shaggy foot, go easy. The protruding strands ignite fast, so rotate the cigar gently during toasting and keep the flame’s distance conservative to avoid scorching the wrapper edge. A cedar spill works particularly well here because its lower, softer flame gives you more control than a hot single-torch jet.

How does foot type affect how I store cigars in my humidor?

Foot type has minimal effect on storage requirements. All three variations store at the same target humidity (65-70% RH) and temperature range. The one practical difference is physical: closed-foot and shaggy-foot cigars are slightly more susceptible to damage at the foot end during handling, since the closed-foot twist can crack if a cigar dries out severely, and the shaggy-foot filler strands can break or compress if stored with the foot pressing against something hard.

When organizing your humidor, especially a full-capacity box or a coolidor, consider orienting all cigars in the same direction with feet away from the container wall. This small habit protects the foot construction and also makes identification easier when you’re logging a session. If you use a cataloging tool like Stick Picks, you can note foot type in your journal entries and track whether a particular construction consistently affects your experience on the first third versus the rest of the smoke.

Does foot type tell me anything about a cigar’s quality or price?

A question we hear often: whether closed-foot construction is a reliable quality signal or just marketing.

Honest answer: foot type is a blending and brand identity decision, not a quality guarantee. Plenty of exceptional open-foot cigars exist across every price tier, and plenty of mediocre cigars have been dressed up with a closed foot for shelf appeal. That said, closed-foot construction does add rolling complexity and time. It requires an extra step and additional skill from the roller, so it genuinely appears more often in premium and ultra-premium lines priced above $15-20 per stick.

Shaggy feet are even less correlated with price. Some of the most affordable boutique Nicaraguan sticks feature a shaggy foot because it’s a natural consequence of how certain tobacco leaves are rolled, not a premium add-on. According to industry insiders familiar with factory floor processes, the shaggy foot is often left intentionally in blends where the blender wants the filler to “speak first” without wrapper interference.

The better signal is your own journal. After a dozen smokes, if you notice that closed-foot cigars consistently deliver a more complex, layered progression for your palate, that’s actionable data worth tracking, regardless of what any rating or price tag says.

Are there any other foot variations I should know about?

Beyond the three main types, you may occasionally encounter a partially closed foot, where the wrapper folds over just part of the foot rather than the full circumference. This creates an asymmetric starting point and requires extra attention during the toast to ensure the fully open side doesn’t ignite before the covered side is ready.

Some limited-edition cigars also feature perfecto-style closed feet, where both ends are tapered and closed in a double-taper shape, making the cigar symmetrical from foot to head. These are rare but striking, and lighting one requires extra patience. Start the toast at the very tip of the taper, then gradually widen your toasting motion outward as the ember forms. Never rush a perfecto foot with full-contact torch flame; the tapered construction concentrates heat quickly and can tunnel before the first draw.

FAQs

What is a closed foot cigar? A closed foot cigar has wrapper-grade tobacco folded over the lighting end, enclosing the filler and binder. You toast it longer than an open foot, and as the wrapper burns away it delivers a pure wrapper flavor in the first third before the full blend opens up.

How do you light a shaggy foot cigar? Toast the protruding filler strands gently and evenly before drawing. Use a torch or a cedar spill and rotate the cigar slowly. Because the loose filler catches quickly, a lighter touch than you’d use on a closed foot is all you need. Avoid scorching the wrapper edge.

Does the foot type affect cigar flavor? Yes. A closed foot front-loads wrapper flavor in the first few puffs. A shaggy foot offers immediate filler character and often a bolder, faster-opening smoke. A standard open foot delivers the blend’s profile from the very first draw with no introductory wrapper layer.

Can you cut a closed foot cigar like a normal cigar? Some smokers do cut the closed foot off, treating it like a standard open foot. It works fine, but you lose the wrapper-only introductory experience the maker intended. The traditional approach is to toast, not cut, the closed end until the wrapper burns back naturally.

Are shaggy foot cigars lower quality? Not at all. The shaggy foot is a deliberate construction choice, common in premium Nicaraguan and Dominican blends. The exposed filler gives an immediate preview of the tobacco character before the wrapper fully integrates, and many collectors prize that opening act.
