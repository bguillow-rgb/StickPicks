How to Taste a Cigar: A Collector's Tasting Guide

By Stick Picks · Published October 6, 2026

Quick Answer

To taste a cigar properly, start with a cold draw after cutting to preview the raw flavor profile. Smoke in thirds, noting the first third's opening notes, the second third's complexity, and the final third's intensity. Pace one puff per minute, never inhale, and retrohale occasionally to engage your full sense of smell.

Cigars are evaluated through a structured sensory experience, not just lit and smoked, but tasted deliberately across their full length. Whether you’re journaling entries in Stick Picks or simply trying to get more out of a stick you paid good money for, understanding how to taste a cigar transforms every session from a habit into an assessment.

What exactly does “tasting” a cigar mean, isn’t smoking enough?

Smoking and tasting are related, but they’re not the same thing. Smoking is passive; tasting is active. When you taste deliberately, you’re evaluating the cigar across several dimensions at once: aroma, flavor, draw resistance, burn quality, retrohale character, and how those qualities shift from one end to the other. Most serious collectors report that active tasting meaningfully deepens their appreciation of a blend and helps them make smarter buying decisions over time.

Think of it like wine. You can just pour and drink, but the people getting the most out of a bottle are the ones who look at the color, nose it before sipping, and pay attention to the finish. Cigar tasting works the same way, just with tobacco instead of grapes, and “thirds” instead of “sips.”

The payoff is practical, too. If you can identify what you like and dislike with precision (“too peppery up front,” “beautiful creaminess in the second third,” “turned harsh in the final inch”), you build a vocabulary that lets you select better cigars and communicate clearly with your tobacconist. According to data from major cigar review platforms, the attributes reviewers score most heavily are flavor complexity, consistency across thirds, and finish, all things an active taster picks up that a passive smoker misses.

How do I do the cold draw, and what am I actually looking for?

A question we hear often:

The cold draw is the act of clipping a cigar and sucking air through it before lighting. It tells you whether the cigar will draw well and gives you a first impression of how it may taste once lit.

In practice: after cutting the cap, place the head in your mouth and draw slowly. You’re noticing two things simultaneously.

Draw resistance. A tight, restricted cold draw signals a difficult smoke ahead. A very loose draw means the cigar was under-filled. Ideal resistance is firm but not labored, like drinking through a cocktail straw, not a coffee stirrer.

Raw flavor. The cold draw can surface subtle notes, sweetness, spice, earthiness, cedar, that hint at what’s coming once you light up. The flavors will shift with combustion, but the cold draw offers a clean, unaltered read on the filler tobacco.

Take two or three cold draws, not just one. The second draw often reveals more than the first as your palate adjusts.

What’s the right way to actually draw smoke during the smoke?

Readers frequently ask:

Don’t inhale. Ever. The whole point is to taste the smoke, not push it into your lungs. Draw, savor, exhale through your mouth or nose.

Once smoke is in your mouth, let it sit for a moment before you exhale. Swirl it around, push it to the back of your palate. That’s where most of the flavor registers.

Pacing is the variable beginners underestimate most. Smoke too fast and you overheat the cigar. The tobacco burns hot, produces excess tars and ammonia, and everything turns bitter. One puff every 60 seconds is what most experienced smokers stick to. Set the cigar down between draws. A resting cigar runs cooler, and a cooler cigar tastes better. That’s really all there is to it.

How does retrohaling fit into tasting, and is it really worth doing?

Without retrohaling, you’re working with basic taste sensations only. With it, you get the full range of aromatic compounds the blend has to offer. It’s worth doing.

The technique: after drawing smoke into your mouth (not your lungs), keep your lips closed and gently push a small amount of smoke out through your nose. Start with a fraction of the draw, maybe 10%, to avoid burning and coughing. It feels strange at first but becomes natural within a few sessions.

You don’t need to retrohale every puff. Many experienced smokers do it every third or fourth draw, enough to track aromatic development without wearing out their nasal passages. It’s particularly useful in the second third, when a blend’s complexity is typically at its peak.

What should I be noticing in each third of the cigar?

This one comes up a lot:

Tasting by thirds is the framework every cigar reviewer uses, and for good reason. Most premium blends are built to evolve across their length.

ThirdWhat to expectKey questions to ask
First thirdOpening notes, draw establishment, initial strengthWhat’s the dominant flavor? Is the draw comfortable?
Second thirdComplexity, core character, transitionsDo new notes emerge? Is the burn even? Any retrohale highlights?
Final thirdIntensification, strength rise, finishDoes it stay clean or turn harsh? When should I set it down?

The first third tends to present the more delicate notes. The middle third is where the blend’s core character shows up. The final third often intensifies, bringing bolder flavors or, depending on quality, occasional harshness.

The second third is where you should do most of your active evaluation. The tobacco is fully lit, the oils are warm, and you have enough smoke behind you to know whether the blend is consistent. Many collectors find that a cigar they were ready to write off in the first third becomes something else entirely in the middle.

In the final third, strength and intensity tend to climb. Common late-stage notes include charred wood, dark chocolate, roasted coffee, and molasses. When harshness starts overtaking flavor, usually in the final inch, set the cigar down. The resins and oils that accumulate near the nub don’t improve anything.

What flavor families should I actually be trying to identify?

The honest answer depends entirely on the cigar. That said, most premium cigars fall into a recognizable set of flavor families. Cedar, leather, earth, coffee, cocoa, nuts, cream, black pepper, and dried fruit are the notes that come up again and again.

Here’s a quick reference:

Flavor familyCommon specific notes
EarthLeather, soil, minerals, barnyard
RoastEspresso, dark chocolate, cocoa, coffee cream
WoodCedar, oak, sawdust, pine
SpiceBlack pepper, white pepper, clove, cinnamon
Sweet/CreamyVanilla, cream, dried fruit, honey, caramel
Floral/HerbalHay, grass, tea, dried flowers

Don’t pressure yourself to nail every note in the first session. Start by identifying which flavor family shows up first rather than hunting for an exact flavor. Once you can reliably place a cigar as earthy versus sweet versus peppery, drilling down to specific notes gets much easier.

How do I capture what I’m tasting so I actually remember it?

Active tasting only compounds in value if you record what you find. Even a few sentences per cigar, what you picked up in each third, draw quality, burn behavior, overall impression, what you’d pair it with, creates a reference library that sharpens your palate over time.

FAQ

What is a cold draw and why does it matter? A cold draw is pulling unlit air through a cut cigar before lighting. It previews the raw tobacco’s flavor, sweetness, spice, cedar, or earthiness, and confirms the draw resistance is right. A very tight draw signals a potential construction issue before you even light up.

Should I inhale cigar smoke to taste it better? No. Cigars are not inhaled. Draw smoke into your mouth, let it coat your palate, swirl it gently, then exhale through your mouth or nose. Inhaling into your lungs produces harshness rather than flavor and significantly increases nicotine absorption.

How often should I puff on a cigar while tasting it? Once every 45-60 seconds is the standard guideline. Smoking faster overheats the tobacco, producing bitter, tarry flavors that mask the blend’s character. Setting the cigar in an ashtray between puffs keeps the burn temperature in the ideal range.

What flavors should I expect from a cigar? Common tasting note families include earth (leather, soil, minerals), roast (coffee, cocoa, espresso), wood (cedar, oak), spice (black pepper, clove), and sweet/creamy (vanilla, cream, dried fruit). The exact notes depend on wrapper origin, blend, and age.

Does retrohaling change what you taste in a cigar? Yes, significantly. Retrohaling, gently exhaling a small amount of smoke through your nose, activates your olfactory receptors, which detect far more aromatic complexity than your tongue alone. Many flavor notes described in cigar reviews are only accessible this way.

Why does my cigar taste bitter or harsh? The most common causes are smoking too fast (overheating the tobacco), relighting a cold dead cigar, or smoking all the way to the very end where resins and tars concentrate. Slow down, let the cigar rest between puffs, and plan to set it down in the final inch.
