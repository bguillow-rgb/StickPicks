What Size Humidor Do I Need? A Collector's Sizing Guide

By Stick Picks · Published June 22, 2026

Quick Answer

The right humidor size holds roughly twice your current collection, plus room to grow. A 25-50 count desktop box suits casual smokers; 75-150 count units work for active collectors; 200+ count cabinets or wineadors are ideal for serious aging projects. Always leave about 20% of space empty for air circulation.

Getting the size wrong is the most common humidor mistake collectors make, and it almost always goes in one direction: too small. You buy a 25-count desktop box, fill it in a month, and spend the next year either cramming cigars in or shopping for a second humidor you never planned to buy. A little sizing math upfront saves a lot of frustration later.

Why do so many people outgrow their first humidor so fast?

A question we hear often:

The short answer is that cigars accumulate faster than most people expect. A sampler becomes a box. A box becomes a rotation. A rotation turns into a modest aging project with a waiting list, and suddenly the desktop humidor that felt spacious six months ago is packed with no room for anything new.

There’s a well-known rule of thumb in the collector community: buy a humidor sized for roughly twice the cigars you own right now. If you keep about 25 sticks on hand, look at 50-count units. If you’re regularly sitting on 50, target the 100-count tier. The logic is simple: collections grow, and upgrading humidors mid-hobby is both expensive and disruptive to cigars already in the seasoning groove.

There’s also an airflow argument. Industry guidance consistently recommends leaving approximately 20% of your humidor’s interior free. That breathing room lets humidity circulate evenly, so the cigars at the back of the box aren’t drier than the ones up front. A perpetually overstuffed humidor fights against itself.

Entry-level desktop units in the $50-$150 range hold up to 50 cigars and handle basic storage duties well. Mid-range units ($150-$500) are the sweet spot for hobbyists who want both performance and presentation. High-end cabinet humidors and electronic systems ($500 and up) are built for serious collectors or anyone running a dedicated aging program.

For collections pushing 200 cigars or beyond, or for anyone serious about long-term aging, a wineador (a temperature-controlled wine cabinet repurposed for cigars) is a popular upgrade. Expect to spend $400-$1,500 depending on size, but the climate stability is hard to match with a passive wood box.

How do capacity ratings actually work, and why don’t they match reality?

This one comes up a lot:

Manufacturers rate humidor capacity based on a standard robusto: roughly 5 inches long with a 50 ring gauge. That’s a moderately sized cigar, not the biggest, not the smallest. If your collection leans toward Churchills, double coronas, or large-ring torpedoes, your real-world capacity will run meaningfully lower than the stated count.

Conversely, if you smoke a lot of petite coronas or lanceros, you may actually fit more than the listed number. Treat the capacity number as a guideline, not a guarantee. When shopping, ask yourself what vitolas you actually smoke most, and mentally adjust from there. Some retailers publish internal dimensions (length x width x depth), which lets you do the math precisely.

One more wrinkle: humidors with multiple trays, dividers, or lift-out drawers use some of that interior volume for hardware. A 100-count box with a tray and divider system might realistically hold 75-85 cigars comfortably.

What’s the difference between a desktop humidor and a cabinet humidor for aging?

For aging projects specifically, size matters in ways beyond just headcount. Full factory boxes, a standard box of 20 or 25 cigars, need horizontal space that most desktop humidors can’t accommodate without removing the tray entirely. If you plan to age cigars in the box they came in (a common and sensible practice, since the box itself contributes cedar-forward aromatics), you’ll want a cabinet-style or coolidor setup with enough shelf depth for a full box to lie flat.

Cabinet humidors also handle the opening-and-closing cycle better. Every time you open a desktop lid, conditioned air escapes. On a small box you’re doing this frequently; on a cabinet with Spanish-cedar-lined drawers, individual compartments insulate the rest of the collection from ambient air exposure. For cigars you’re aging a year or more, that stability compounds over time into genuinely better results.

That said, a well-sealed mid-size desktop unit with quality humidification, whether Boveda packs or a bead system, can age cigars beautifully for 1-3 years. The key variables are seal quality, consistent temperature, and not opening it constantly out of curiosity.

Should I start with one humidor or two?

Readers frequently ask:

Two humidors is a surprisingly practical setup for collectors who smoke regularly and age simultaneously. Aging cigars benefit from slightly lower humidity, around 63-65% RH, while cigars in active rotation smoke best at 65-70% RH. Trying to split the difference in one box means neither group is optimally stored.

A common two-humidor approach: keep a modest 50-75 count desktop humidor for your current rotation (the cigars you’re smoking within the next few months), and a larger, cooler cabinet or coolidor for your aging stock. Label everything clearly with purchase date and target aging window. Apps like Stick Picks make this easy. You can catalog each cigar, note which humidor it lives in, and flag it with a target date so nothing gets forgotten at the back of a shelf for a decade.

What’s the one mistake collectors most regret about humidor sizing?

Buying too small is the universal regret, and it’s the lesson most people say they learned on their second humidor purchase. A few sticks from a sampler becomes a box; a box becomes a rotation; and within a year many new collectors have more cigars than their first humidor can responsibly hold.

The countermove is simple: when you’re deciding between two size tiers, go up. The incremental cost is usually modest, and a humidor with room to grow beats one that’s perpetually overpacked. Overcrowding restricts airflow, makes rotating cigars without disturbing everything else much harder, and can create humidity hot spots where some sticks get too much moisture. Leaving roughly 20% of the space open isn’t wasted capacity. It’s proper environmental management.

If you’re just starting out and unsure of your long-term smoking pace, a quality 75-100 count mid-size desktop is almost always the better opening move over a 25-count starter box. You’ll grow into it, and it will serve you for years rather than months.

How does my local climate affect which humidor I should buy?

Climate is an underrated factor. In consistently temperate, moderate-humidity regions, a passive wooden humidor with a reliable humidification system will maintain target RH with minimal intervention. In very dry climates, high desert, arid interiors, the humidor has to work harder to retain moisture, and passive sponge or foam humidifiers often can’t keep up. A Boveda pack system or a bead-based humidifier matters more in these conditions.

In humid coastal climates, the challenge flips: your humidor may absorb ambient moisture and trend high. Active two-way humidity control handles this better than systems that only add humidity. Boveda packs operate bidirectionally, adding or absorbing moisture as needed, which makes them a good fit on either extreme.

Temperature is the other climate variable worth taking seriously. Tobacco beetles, a genuine threat to any collection, become active around 72-74 degrees F and above. If your storage space gets warm in summer, a temperature-controlled wineador isn’t a luxury; it’s a preventative measure. At minimum, keep your humidor in a climate-controlled room away from windows and heat sources, regardless of box type.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many cigars does a ‘50-count’ humidor actually hold? Manufacturer capacity ratings are based on a standard robusto (roughly 5 x 50 ring gauge). If your collection skews toward larger vitolas, Churchills, double coronas, or big-ring gordos, your real-world capacity will be noticeably lower than the stated count. Budget for at least 20-25% fewer cigars than the listed number when storing larger formats.

Is it bad to have a humidor that’s too big for my cigars? A nearly empty humidor can be harder to stabilize because there’s more air volume and fewer cigars contributing moisture. A humidor that’s 50-70% full is perfectly fine. Just avoid leaving it completely bare. Fill empty space with cedar sheets, a Spanish cedar divider tray, or prop cigars until your collection grows.

Do I need a wineador or coolidor for aging? Not necessarily. A well-sealed desktop humidor can age cigars effectively for 1-3 years. But if you’re storing more than 100-150 cigars, aging full factory boxes, or living somewhere with dramatic seasonal temperature swings, a temperature-controlled wineador offers meaningfully better climate stability.

Can I keep two smaller humidors instead of upgrading to one large one? Yes, and many collectors prefer it. Two humidors let you run different humidity targets, say 65% RH for aging and 69% RH for your smoking rotation, without compromise. Each unit needs its own calibrated hygrometer to monitor conditions independently.

What wood should my humidor be lined with? Spanish cedar is the industry standard. It’s hygroscopic, naturally helps repel tobacco beetles, and contributes a classic aroma that complements aged tobacco. Avoid pine, particle board, or unlined interiors. They can leach resins or fail to regulate moisture properly over time.
