Cigar Tubes in Your Humidor: Cap On, Off, or Remove?

By Stick Picks · Published September 17, 2026

Quick Answer

Yes, tubed cigars still need a humidor. Aluminum tubes are not airtight and will only preserve freshness for a few days outside one. Inside a humidor, you have three options: tube sealed, tube cap off, or cigar removed entirely. Your choice depends on how soon you plan to smoke and whether you want faster or slower aging.

Tubed cigars are a staple of the premium humidor, whether you picked them up at a B&M, received a gifted Davidoff, or pulled a tubo from a sampler box. Yet a surprisingly underserved question keeps appearing on forums and in lounge conversations: what exactly do you do with that tube once the cigar is inside your humidor? Cap on? Cap off? Ditch the tube entirely? The answer matters more than you might think.

Do tubed cigars actually need a humidor, or is the tube enough?

A question we hear often: collectors assume the tube acts like a tiny humidor. It doesn’t.

A tube will only maintain a cigar’s humidity for so long, usually no more than a few days under normal conditions, and aluminum tubos are not airtight. That means any cigar sitting in an unsealed aluminum tube in a dry room is slowly losing moisture, just a little more slowly than a bare cigar would. Glass tubes, while offering an initially tighter seal, are not designed for long-term storage either. Over time the seal can allow slight air exchange, and gradual moisture loss follows.

The practical upshot: every tubed cigar, aluminum, glass, or plastic, belongs in a humidor if you’re keeping it for more than a week. Cello wrap, no cello, tubos, all of it. The tube is protective packaging, not a storage solution.

So what is a cigar tube actually for?

While the tubo might look like it’s intended to keep the cigar fresh, its main purpose is protecting your cigar from physical damage, making it easy to carry without worrying about dinging it up.

Manufacturers put certain selections in tubes for reasons ranging from marketing a new line extension, to making the cigar easier to carry when you go out, to providing better protection than a cellophane wrapper.

Most premium cigars packaged in an aluminum tube are wrapped in a cedar sleeve to better encapsulate flavor and create a barrier from the aluminum. That cedar sleeve is worth paying attention to. It does quiet aging work even inside the tube, imparting subtle woody character and absorbing minor humidity fluctuations. The tube is a vessel; the cedar sleeve is the real value for collectors.

Tubes are also genuinely good for one specific use case: gifting. Their individual packaging makes them ideal for recipients who may not have proper storage conditions but want to buy a cigar to smoke at a later date.

Inside my humidor, should I store the tube with the cap on, cap off, or remove the cigar entirely?

This one comes up a lot, and there’s no single universal answer. It depends on your goals.

You have options: put the tube in a humidor still sealed, remove the lid and place the open tube in the humidor, or abandon the tube and place the cigar alone in the humidor. Which way you go comes down to when you’re planning to smoke it.

Here’s a practical breakdown:

Tube cap removedModerateModerateMedium-term storage (1-3 years), balanced aging
Cigar removed from tubeFullFastestStandard humidor aging, smoking within 1-2 years
Cigar removed, tube savedFullFastestDaily rotation stock; keep tube for travel later

A common approach among experienced collectors is to remove the cigar completely and set the tube aside for travel, or to put the tubo in the humidor with the cap removed to promote better airflow while keeping the wrapper leaf protected.

How does a sealed tube change the way a cigar ages?

This is where the topic gets genuinely interesting for serious collectors.

A cigar matures more slowly in a tube than when it’s lying unwrapped in a humidor. Almost no fresh air gets to a cigar in a sealed tube, so the aromas don’t develop as quickly and the cigar holds its fragrance longer. In wine terms, this is analogous to the difference between oxidative and reductive aging. Opened cigars mature together in the humidor; tubed cigars develop sealed off almost entirely from air, what amounts to reductive storage with near-isolation from oxygen.

The practical consequence: if you want to smoke a cigar in the coming year or two and want it to mature, remove it from the tube to quicken aroma development. If you’re in no rush and want to leave it for several years, keeping it in the tube can have interesting effects. Stronger cigars left to mature sealed can become noticeably milder.

This makes sealed-tube storage a legitimate long-term strategy for full-bodied, high-strength blends. One collector account describes Cuban tubos from 1971, stored sealed in aluminum, that were still producing smooth, creamy, pleasantly mild smokes nearly 50 years later. The sealed tube slows aging down, but a lot more of the oils are preserved while sealed. It’s not for every cigar or every collector, but it’s a real technique worth knowing.

Can leaving a sealed tube in a humid humidor cause problems?

Readers frequently ask whether a sealed tube traps too much moisture and causes mold.

You can leave them as is, unless cigars are over-humidified, in which case you can invite mold. The way around this is to remove the cap and store the cigars in the humidor; after a few months, you can replace the cap if you wish.

That’s the one practical risk of sealed-tube storage: if your RH is running high, above 72%, a sealed tube can trap excess moisture against the wrapper. Keep your humidor in the 65-70% range (good practice for aging generally, according to most long-term collectors) and sealed tubes become much less of a concern. If you run a tighter humidity setup and rotate your cigars regularly, sealed tubes inside a well-maintained humidor are generally safe.

What about aluminum vs. glass vs. wooden tubes, does material matter?

Some cigars are packaged in wood, glass, or aluminum tubes, with aluminum being the most common.

Corked or sealed glass tubes may retain freshness a little longer than aluminum, but left out of the box they too will dry out. And if left under a lamp or in sunlight, you risk baking the cigar inside the glass. Wooden tubes (less common today, but found on some high-end releases) breathe the most naturally and are the closest to simply aging in an open humidor, since the wood itself is slightly porous. Aluminum is the workhorse: durable, light, and protective for transport, but not meaningfully breathable. Glass is elegant, often used for presentation and gifting, but fragile and heat-sensitive.

For day-to-day humidor storage with plans to smoke within 1-2 years, material matters far less than simply getting the cap off and letting humidity circulate.

How long will a tubed cigar stay fresh outside a humidor if I’m taking it to an event?

Elegant silver or wooden tubes can keep individual cigars properly moisturized for up to 72 hours. Aluminum tubes perform similarly. That’s a comfortable window for a round of golf, a wedding, a weekend trip, or an evening out, which is exactly what the tube format was designed for. A cigar in a tube will hold its humidity longer than a bare cigar pulled from the same humidor at the same time, but keeping cigars out of a humidified environment unnecessarily isn’t a good habit even if they’re in tubes. They’ll last longer, but only by a matter of days.

If you’re heading somewhere for longer than three days, a small travel humidor is the better call.

FAQs

Do cigars in tubes still need to be stored in a humidor? Yes. Aluminum and glass tubes are not airtight and will only maintain freshness for a few days at most outside a humidified environment. All tubed cigars should be kept in a humidor for any storage beyond a week.

Should I store a tubed cigar with the cap on or off inside a humidor? Cap off (or cigar removed entirely) is best for most collectors. Leaving the cap sealed slows humidity exchange significantly, which also slows aging. Removing the cap lets moisture and air circulate normally while the tube still protects the wrapper.

Does leaving a cigar in its tube affect how it ages? Yes, noticeably. A sealed tube drastically limits airflow, slowing flavor development and keeping the cigar’s fresh aroma intact longer. This “reductive” aging produces a different, often milder long-term result than open-air aging in the humidor.

What type of cigar tube is best for short-term travel storage? Aluminum tubes with a cedar sleeve insert are the most common and practical for travel. They protect the wrapper from physical damage and slow moisture loss for up to 72 hours outside a humidor, enough for a day trip or event.

Is there any benefit to aging a cigar inside a sealed tube long-term? Potentially, yes, for strong, full-bodied blends. A sealed tube isolates the cigar from oxygen, so oils are better preserved over many years. Some collectors have reported very strong cigars mellowing pleasantly after 10-15+ years in sealed aluminum tubes.
