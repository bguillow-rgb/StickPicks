Best Humidor Alternatives for Cigars (Ranked & Compared)

By Stick Picks · Published September 5, 2026

Quick Answer

The best humidor alternatives for cigars are, in order of capability: a tupperdor (airtight plastic container + Boveda pack), a coolidor (converted cooler), a mason jar, and a Ziploc bag with a humidity pack. Each works by maintaining 65, 72% RH. None require seasoning, unlike a traditional wooden humidor.

Whether you just received a handful of sticks as a gift, you’ve outgrown your desktop humidor, or you want to test the hobby before spending several hundred dollars on a cedar box, a proper wooden humidor is not your only option. Several alternatives hold cigars at the correct relative humidity (RH) just as reliably, and some experienced collectors swear by plastic containers over traditional wood for long-term aging.

The key insight: cigar storage performance comes from conditions, not from what the container looks like. Maintain 65, 72% RH and keep temperature stable below 73°F (23°C), and your cigars won’t know the difference between a $400 desktop humidor and a $12 plastic food container.

Why would anyone use a humidor alternative in the first place?

A question we hear often:

Three reasons come up again and again. First, cost: a quality wooden humidor with reliable humidity control routinely runs $100, $500 or more, which is a steep entry point when you’re still figuring out whether you’ll stick with the hobby. Second, capacity: many desktop humidors seal poorly when overfilled, and a plastic coolidor or tupperdor holds more cigars per dollar than most wood boxes in the same price range. Third, convenience: airtight plastic containers require no seasoning and hold humidity more consistently than many beginner wooden humidors, whose seals loosen over time.

That said, alternatives are not a permanent substitute for every collector. If you’re aging a serious investment, rare limited-edition sticks or boxes you plan to hold for five or more years, Spanish cedar’s natural properties (moisture regulation, trace oils, mild beetle deterrence) add genuine value that plastic alone doesn’t replicate without modification.

How does a tupperdor actually work, and is it as good as people claim?

This one comes up a lot:

A tupperdor is any airtight food-grade plastic container pressed into service as a cigar humidor. Rubbermaid, Lock & Lock, and IRIS are favorites. The plastic creates a near-perfect seal that many traditional entry-level wooden humidors can’t match out of the box without modification.

Setup takes under five minutes. Place your cigars inside, add a Boveda humidity pack at your target RH (65%, 69%, or 72% are the standard options), close the lid, and store the container somewhere cool and dark, away from direct sunlight. A small digital hygrometer tucked inside lets you verify conditions at a glance. Because plastic doesn’t absorb moisture, the Boveda pack does all the work maintaining stable RH rather than first hydrating wood walls, which means your cigars reach proper condition faster.

The one trade-off is airflow. Plastic containers are so airtight that opening the lid briefly every two to three weeks to exchange fresh air is worth doing. Without occasional air circulation, the environment inside can turn stale over long periods. Two or three Spanish cedar strips address both that concern and provide the cedar conditioning benefits a plastic box otherwise lacks. Many collectors who own traditional humidors use a tupperdor as a secondary overflow vessel for boxes they’re not actively smoking from. It’s genuinely that effective at holding condition.

What about a coolidor, and when does it make sense over a tupperdor?

A coolidor is a standard insulated beverage cooler, think Igloo or Coleman, converted into cigar storage by adding a humidity source and a digital hygrometer. The insulation does real work here: it buffers temperature swings from room to room and season to season, which matters for long-term aging stability.

The main reason to choose a coolidor over a tupperdor is scale. A tupperdor tops out at a few hundred loose cigars before you’re stacking boxes awkwardly. A 48-quart cooler can comfortably hold dozens of boxes in their original packaging, a practical consideration for collectors who buy by the box regularly. The other advantage is thermal mass: because a cooler is insulated, it maintains a more stable internal temperature even in warm summers. Temperature spikes above 73°F accelerate the risk of tobacco beetle hatching, so that insulation provides real protection.

Coolidor setups work best with multiple Boveda packs (or a dedicated electronic humidifier for very large coolers), cedar trays or strips for conditioning, and a reliable digital hygrometer. Some collectors line the bottom of their coolidor with old cigar boxes to add cedar surface area without additional cost.

Can a mason jar actually keep cigars fresh?

Yes. For smaller quantities, a sealed glass jar is one of the most effective cigar storage vessels available. Glass doesn’t absorb or off-gas odors, creates a genuinely airtight seal, and lets you see your cigars without opening the container. A wide-mouth quart mason jar holds roughly five to eight robusto-sized cigars comfortably; a half-gallon jar can accommodate ten to fifteen.

The setup mirrors a tupperdor exactly: add a small Boveda pack or a piece of damp sponge (always use distilled water, since tap water can introduce bacteria that promote mold), arrange your cigars so they don’t contact the moisture source directly, seal, and store in a dark location. The main limitation is capacity. If you own more than a box’s worth of cigars, you’ll need multiple jars, which starts to feel unwieldy. But for a gift batch of five or six sticks, or a small personal rotation, a mason jar is a clean solution, and if you already have one in the kitchen, the cost is zero.

Is a Ziploc bag with a Boveda pack actually viable for real storage?

Readers frequently ask:

More viable than most people expect. A quality zip-closure bag is essentially airtight, and a correctly-sized Boveda pack does the work of a traditional humidifier with no maintenance required. For short-to-medium-term storage, a few days to a few weeks, this is a completely legitimate method. Many brick-and-mortar cigar shops hand customers their singles in a Ziploc sleeve with a Boveda pack already inside, effectively using this method commercially.

A few nuances worth knowing: use a Boveda pack sized for the volume of tobacco in the bag (a 4-gram pack works for a few cigars; an 8-gram pack for up to ten or so). Squeeze excess air out of the bag before sealing to reduce the humidity buffer your pack has to overcome. Keep the bag away from direct sunlight and heat sources, because plastic bags offer no insulation whatsoever, which makes temperature management entirely dependent on where you store them. For anything longer than a month, upgrade to a tupperdor or coolidor. A Ziploc bag’s seal is sufficient for short storage but not built for the consistent long-term performance of a purpose-built airtight container.

Does using plastic instead of Spanish cedar affect how cigars taste or age?

This is the honest question most “alternatives” articles sidestep. For storage up to two or three years, the difference is negligible if you add cedar to your plastic setup. Without cedar strips, some collectors notice that cigars stored exclusively in plastic for extended periods develop a slightly flat aromatic profile compared to cedar-aged sticks. The cedar oils contribute subtle complexity over time that plastic can’t replicate on its own.

The practical fix is easy. A bundle of Spanish cedar strips, available from most cigar retailers for a few dollars, dropped inside any tupperdor or coolidor adds that character back. For long-term aging of your most prized sticks, anything you’re holding for five years or more, a dedicated cedar-lined environment (a proper humidor, wineador, or cedar-trayed coolidor) is worth the investment. For everything else, plastic with cedar strips performs on par with a traditional humidor in blind taste comparisons, according to experienced collectors across major cigar forums.

How do I monitor humidity inside any of these alternatives?

Every storage vessel, whether it’s a $5 mason jar or a $50 coolidor, benefits from a small digital hygrometer. Analog hygrometers require calibration and tend to drift; a digital model reads accurately out of the box and typically costs $10, $20. Place it inside your storage container before sealing, then check it periodically rather than relying on your humidity source alone to indicate conditions.

That kind of provenance data is genuinely useful for dialing in your preferred storage setup over time, especially if you’re running a tupperdor and a wooden humidor simultaneously.

Which alternative should I actually start with?

For most people entering the hobby with fewer than fifty cigars: start with a tupperdor. The setup cost is under $25 including a Boveda pack, it requires no preparation time, it holds humidity more reliably than many beginner wooden humidors, and it scales by simply adding a second container when you outgrow the first. Add a couple of Spanish cedar strips and a small digital hygrometer and you have a storage setup that experienced collectors genuinely respect, regardless of what it looks like on a shelf.

If your collection regularly exceeds three to four boxes in active storage, step up to a coolidor. If you’re storing individual sticks short-term or assembling a gift, a mason jar or Ziploc with Boveda gets the job done with zero infrastructure. A traditional wooden humidor earns its place when display matters, when you’re aging for the long term, or when you want the full ritual experience of the hobby. But it’s a want, not a need, for good cigar preservation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can cigars stay fresh in a tupperdor?

A well-maintained tupperdor, an airtight plastic container with a Boveda or silica bead humidity source, can keep cigars fresh and properly humidified for a year or more. Swap out Boveda packs every 2, 3 months and open the lid briefly every few weeks to refresh the air.

Do I need to season a tupperdor or coolidor like a wooden humidor?

No. Plastic and glass containers don't absorb moisture the way Spanish cedar does, so there's no seasoning step needed. Just add a calibrated humidity source (a Boveda pack or silica beads) and you're ready to store cigars immediately.

Can I add cedar to a tupperdor or coolidor?

Yes, and many collectors do. A few Spanish cedar strips or a small cedar tray placed inside your tupperdor or coolidor adds the natural cedar conditioning benefits, light insect deterrence, mild flavor enhancement, without requiring a full wooden humidor.

Is a Ziploc bag with a Boveda pack safe for long-term cigar storage?

A Ziploc bag with a Boveda pack is safe for several weeks of short-to-medium-term storage, and experienced collectors have reported smoking cigars stored this way for years. However, for serious aging or large collections, an airtight container (tupperdor or coolidor) offers more stability and capacity.

What humidity level should I maintain in a cigar humidor alternative?

Target 65, 72% relative humidity (RH) in any cigar storage vessel. Most serious collectors settle on 65, 69% RH for consistent burning and aging. Pair your humidity source with a small digital hygrometer so you can verify actual conditions inside your container.
