Cigar Cap Types: Flat, Triple, Pigtail & More Explained

By Stick Picks · Published October 14, 2026

Quick Answer

Cigar caps come in five main types: flat (single), double, triple, flag, and pigtail. Each seals the wrapper at the head, signals where to cut, and reflects the roller's craftsmanship. Knowing the difference helps you cut more precisely, preserve the draw, and appreciate what premium construction actually looks like in the hand.

When most collectors talk about “what’s at the head of a cigar,” they mean the cap: that small, purposeful piece of wrapper leaf that seals everything in place, tells you where to cut, and signals quite a lot about the roller’s skill. Not all caps are the same, and knowing the difference genuinely changes how you approach every cigar in your humidor.

What exactly is a cigar cap, and why does it matter?

The cap is the small piece of wrapper leaf applied to the head of a cigar to seal the construction and hold the wrapper in place. It’s the part you cut before smoking, and a properly placed, intact cap is what keeps the draw clean and the wrapper from going anywhere.

Without a cap, the wrapper would unravel the moment you put a flame near it. But the cap does more than hold things together. Applying it is the final step in the rolling process, after the buncher assembles the filler tobaccos and the roller encloses the bunch in the wrapper leaf. That makes the cap the last signature a torcedor puts on a cigar. The technique they use reflects their training, the factory’s tradition, and sometimes the specific brand’s identity.

From a collector’s standpoint, the cap type also tells you how to cut and how the cigar was meant to smoke. Get it wrong, cut too deep, cut the wrong style, or tear a tight triple cap off cold, and you’ve compromised a cigar you may have been aging for years.

What are the main cigar cap types I’ll actually encounter?

A question we hear often: collectors who’ve been in the hobby a while know what a triple cap looks like on paper but aren’t always sure how to identify it by feel in the humidor. Here’s a practical breakdown of every cap type you’ll encounter.

The five main types: the single cap (one circular piece of wrapper, common in machine-made and budget cigars), the double cap (two wrapper pieces, more protection, found across most premium handmades), the triple cap (three pieces, a sign of expert craftsmanship, typically Cuban and high-end non-Cuban), the flag cap (wrapper twisted or folded over the head, found on some artisanal or limited cigars), and the pigtail cap (a twisted “tail” on top that is both decorative and functional, seen on boutique or handmade cigars).

The double cap is where most of the premium market lives. The flag-and-cap method covers the head with a flag, then adds a circular cap on top for a finished, reinforced look. Most premium cigars use this finish.

The triple cap takes it one step further. The traditional Cuban method is meticulous. Two extra pieces of wrapper leaf close the head, leaving three visible seams. While the cigar is being wrapped, the flag is left hanging near the head. Then a teardrop-shaped piece of wrapper is woven into the flag to cover the head, and a final cap is cut from excess leaf using a casquillo and placed on top with natural vegetable gum.

What’s actually different about a pigtail cap — is it just decorative?

This one comes up a lot: collectors who encounter their first pigtail cap aren’t always sure whether to cut it or just pull it off.

The pigtail is a thin strip of tobacco that protrudes from the head of the cigar, usually twisted, anywhere from a quarter inch to an inch or longer. There’s ongoing debate over whether the twisted cap is purely aesthetic or serves a practical purpose, since it can be twisted off by hand, removing the need for a cutter entirely.

In practice, both things are true. The pigtail is a finishing technique rooted in classic Cuban rolling traditions. Some formats, like the Lancero, have a flag at the head where the headpiece is twisted, the excess wrapper earning the nickname pigtail or curly head.

A pigtail can result in a tighter draw. You can twist and pull it off by hand, though a guillotine cutter is the safer move if you’re not practiced with your fingers. The risk of twisting by hand: if the gum has fully set, especially on a humidor-aged cigar, you can pull too hard and crack the wrapper at the shoulder. When in doubt, cut just below the twist. You lose the tail but keep the head intact.

How do I cut a triple cap without wrecking it?

Readers frequently ask: the triple cap feels intimidating precisely because it’s clearly been assembled with care. But it cuts no differently from a double cap. What changes is where you position the blade.

The principle is the same across all round-head caps: find the shoulder (the slightly rounded area where the cigar starts to curve), cut just above it to preserve the cap and prevent unraveling, and use the right tool. Guillotine and V-cutters offer precision; punch cutters only work on rounded heads. Don’t cut into the body or below the cap line.

On a triple cap specifically, you’ll feel a slightly thicker, firmer head than on a single cap. Three layers of leaf and vegetable gum. Use a sharp guillotine and make one decisive motion. Hesitating mid-cut or sawing back and forth is how you fray the wrapper edge. A well-cut triple cap should leave a clean ring opening of roughly 18-20mm on a standard robusto gauge. If you use Stick Picks to log your smokes, noting the cap type in your journal entry is worth the few seconds it takes. Cut quality consistently shapes how a session begins.

What about figurado caps — torpedos, pyramids, and belicosos?

The figurado family deserves its own category because the cap concept changes shape entirely. Figurados are a broad category, and the most common have a pointed head, which requires different finishing techniques.

Pyramid cigars feature fine-tipped heads similar to torpedos but with sides that aren’t straight, and their ring gauges can increase by more than 10 from head to foot. They’re known for controlled burn and a concentrated flavor delivery from the tapered draw.

For torpedos and pyramids, cut at the shoulder: the point where the taper begins to widen into the body, not at the very tip. Cutting at the tip gives too small an opening and a punishing draw. Cutting too far down removes the structural taper that gives the vitola its characteristic smoke concentration. One shape that bends these rules is the Chisel, like the La Flor Dominicana Air Bender. For that style, you can use a punch on the flat sides, or pinch the sides until an opening appears.

Does the cap type tell me anything about quality or origin?

Yes, consistently. The triple cap is the most reliable quality signal in this category. Cuban-style caps, known as mounted heads, three-seam caps, or triple caps, have three seams and require real labor to execute. When torcedores finish the wrapper application, they take a dollop of natural pectin or vegetable gum and affix scraps of matching wrapper leaf to the shoulder and head of the cigar, sealing the wrapper in place, layer by layer.

A clean, symmetrical triple cap takes significantly more time and skill than a single cap. That’s why you see it on Cohiba, Montecristo, and other flagship Cuban lines, as well as high-end non-Cubans that want to signal comparable craftsmanship. The current trend among boutique manufacturers is elaborate single or dual bands covering most of the cigar, often paired with equally elaborate cap work as part of the premium presentation package.

A sloppy cap, regardless of type, is worth noting in your tasting journal. Wrapper peeling at the shoulder, a cap that rolls off when you handle a cold cigar, or visible gaps in a supposed triple cap are genuine construction red flags. Any of them can predict draw or burn problems before you ever light up.

How does knowing cap types help me as a collector?

Readers frequently ask: why does this matter beyond the moment of cutting?

Three practical payoffs. First, cutting accuracy: knowing which cap you’re working with prevents cutting too deep (unraveling) or too shallow (tight draw). Second, humidor care. A damaged cap during storage compounds problems before you ever light up, since cap condition directly affects how a cigar burns, tastes, and draws. Third, provenance and cataloging. If you track your collection in an app like Stick Picks, logging cap type alongside vitola, wrapper, and origin builds a richer reference for understanding what you own and which construction traditions you actually prefer.

The largest cigar band collection ever assembled was that of American collector Joe Hruby, listed in the Guinness Book of World Records for over 165,000 distinct band varieties. Cigar band collecting is formally called vitolphilia. Cap observation is the quieter cousin of that same attention: a way of reading the cigar in your hand before a single match is struck.

FAQs: Cigar cap types

What is the most common cigar cap type? The double cap is the most common finish on premium handmade cigars today. It uses a flag plus a circular piece of wrapper leaf glued on top, offering a clean look and reliable structural integrity across a wide range of vitolas.

What is a triple cap cigar? A triple cap is a Cuban-origin technique that applies three layers of wrapper leaf to the head: a flag, a teardrop insert woven into the flag, and a final circular cap on top. It’s considered a mark of expert craftsmanship and is common on Cuban Habanos and high-end non-Cuban cigars.

Can you twist off a pigtail cap instead of cutting it? Yes. A pigtail cap can be twisted and gently pulled off by hand. If the tail is short or the glue is tight, using a guillotine cutter just below the twisted section gives a cleaner, more consistent draw opening and avoids tearing the wrapper.

Does the cap type affect the flavor or draw? Cap type primarily affects draw consistency and the cutting experience rather than flavor directly. A tight triple cap maintains structural integrity longer in the humidor. A flag cap creates a very concentrated opening. A poorly cut cap of any type can cause loose draw, unraveling, or tunneling.

How do I know where to cut a torpedo or pyramid cap? On tapered figurado caps (torpedo, pyramid, belicoso), cut at or just above the shoulder: the point where the taper begins to widen toward the body. This leaves enough of the taper intact to concentrate smoke while opening a draw of roughly 18-20mm in diameter.
