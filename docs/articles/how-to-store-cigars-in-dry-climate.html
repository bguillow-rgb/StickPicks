How to Store Cigars in a Dry Climate: The Full Guide

By Stick Picks · Published October 16, 2026

Quick Answer

In a dry climate, cigars need an airtight container (tupperdor or sealed wood humidor), a two-way humidity pack set to 69, 72%, and a digital hygrometer checked weekly. Traditional wooden humidors lose moisture rapidly to parched air, airtight storage is the single most important upgrade you can make.

Dry air is a cigar collector’s quiet nemesis. Whether you’re in Phoenix, Denver, Las Vegas, or Albuquerque, you’ve probably noticed the same thing: you set up what seemed like a perfectly calibrated humidor, and two weeks later the hygrometer reads 55% and your wrappers are starting to feel papery.

Ambient humidity in a heated room can vary anywhere between 10 and 50 percent, and in the desert Southwest, outdoor RH on a hot summer afternoon can drop below 10%. Your humidor isn’t broken. It’s just fighting a war on two fronts.

This guide is for collectors in genuinely arid environments who want a storage system that actually holds up, not a generic “keep it at 70%” answer.

Why does my humidor lose humidity so fast out here?

A question we hear often: collectors in dry states buy a well-reviewed desktop humidor, season it properly, dial in a 69% Boveda, and then watch the reading slide down within days.

A humidor is basically a microclimate. While it can be affected by ambient temperature and humidity, the goal is to create as ideal a condition for cigars inside that box, regardless of where that box may happen to be. The catch is that maintaining that microclimate costs energy, specifically moisture. Traditional wooden humidors constantly lose moisture to the surrounding air, forcing your humidification system to work overtime.

Spanish cedar, the standard lining material in quality humidors, is prized because it buffers moisture, absorbing and releasing it gradually. But that buffering works both ways: in a 20% RH room, the wood keeps trying to equilibrate with the environment around it, slowly bleeding humidity out through every seam and pore. The drier the ambient air, the faster the bleed. A humidor that holds steady for weeks in coastal Florida might need attention twice a week in Tucson.

The fix is not simply adding more humidification. Reduce the rate of loss first, then scale your humidification source to match what actually escapes.

What’s the right RH target for a desert collection?

In very dry climates, staying closer to 65, 69% helps prevent wrappers from becoming brittle without overloading the cigars with moisture. That said, most dry-climate collectors find that targeting 69, 72% inside the humidor gives them a practical buffer. If the RH drifts down between maintenance checks, they land around 66, 68% rather than below 65%, where wrappers start to suffer.

A 72% RH pack is appropriate for high-altitude, arid climates or shipping protection. Think of 72% not as an aggressive setting but as a deliberate safety margin, one that accounts for the moisture your storage vessel will inevitably surrender to the dry air around it.

One caveat worth taking seriously: the conventional wisdom of keeping cigars between 63, 72% RH still holds, especially because raising temperature too far above 70°F creates real tobacco beetle risk. 72% RH paired with 73°F is a combination to avoid. In the desert Southwest, summer temperatures indoors can climb quickly. Prioritize a cool storage location first; humidity adjustments come second.

Airtight vs. wood humidor: which should I use in an arid climate?

This one comes up a lot: collectors already own a quality wood humidor and don’t want to give it up. The good news is that wood humidors can work in dry climates, they just need more support.

Here’s the honest comparison:

In dry climates, airtight containers are generally the most effective option. A sealed container dramatically reduces humidity loss and keeps conditions stable. If you’re starting from scratch, a tupperdor or coolidor gives you the most stable environment for the least effort. If you’re committed to a wood humidor, focus on improving its seal: wax the exterior edge with humidor seal tape or food-safe wax, and check whether the lid closes with gentle resistance all the way around.

Use food-safe containers with tight gaskets, add cedar trays or dividers if you want, and pair them with quality humidity packs and a digital hygrometer. The cedar aroma that comes with a lined humidor is worth pursuing. Just add a raw Spanish cedar plank or tray inside an airtight container to get the best of both.

How many humidity packs do I actually need?

Readers frequently ask: “I already have one 60g Boveda in there, why isn’t it enough?”

Boveda and similar two-way packs are rated by the amount of moisture they can hold and release, not by the size of the space they can service indefinitely. In a humid climate, one 60g pack for a 50-count humidor works fine because the environment isn’t constantly pulling moisture out. In a dry climate, the pack is fighting harder and will exhaust faster.

A practical baseline: one 60g pack per 25 cigars, then add one extra pack if you live somewhere with ambient RH consistently below 35%. A 50-count humidor in Phoenix might run two or three 60g packs rather than one. If you live somewhere the ambient humidity drops below 45% RH, you need to be more deliberate about this.

Replace packs when they feel firm or rigid. In a dry climate, a 60g pack that might last six months in Miami could last six to eight weeks in Albuquerque. Budget accordingly and keep a two-pack backup on hand.

Does altitude matter, or is it just dryness?

Both, and they interact. High-altitude cities like Denver (5,280 ft) and Santa Fe (7,000 ft) are dry because the thinner air holds less water vapor. Boveda’s 72% pack is built for drafty humidors and dry environments. If you live in a high-desert climate (Denver, Phoenix, Albuquerque) and your humidor is a larger wood cabinet that breathes more than a tupperdor, 72% gives you enough of a buffer to stay above 65% during seasonal swings.

Altitude also affects how you smoke. At elevation, cigars tend to draw slightly more freely because there’s less atmospheric pressure, which can make an already well-rolled cigar feel a touch looser. Some collectors store slightly lower (65, 67%) for day-smokes to keep burn character crisp, then keep aging stock at 69, 70%. If you track your smokes in an app like Stick Picks, you can journal observations about draw and burn alongside the date and storage conditions. Over a dry Colorado winter, that data is genuinely useful when you’re dialing in your setup.

What’s my weekly maintenance routine in a dry climate?

Consistency matters more than precision. Here’s a simple weekly check:

  1. Read the hygrometer — note the RH and temperature. If using a digital unit, make sure it’s been salt-calibrated within the last year. (See our guide on how to calibrate a hygrometer.)
  2. Squeeze the humidity packs — a soft, pliable pack still has moisture to give. A stiff, almost crinkly pack needs replacing.
  3. Feel a cigar — gently pinch the body near the foot. It should give slightly and spring back. If it feels like dry cardboard, act immediately.
  4. Rotate position — after any humidity adjustment, reload your cigars in the opposite order from which they were removed. Rotating cigars on a weekly basis helps maintain balanced humidity levels throughout the humidor.
  5. Log the reading — even a quick note in a cigar journal (or your Stick Picks humidor log) gives you a baseline to spot trends before they become problems.

In winter especially, central heating and forced-air systems can drop indoor humidity to 15, 25%. Keeping your cigars humidified matters, particularly in cold-weather or desert climates where exposure to dry air can affect cigars faster than you’d expect. A whole-room humidifier in your storage area can meaningfully reduce the workload on your humidor’s humidification system.

Can I recover cigars that dried out during a humidity drop?

Yes, but slowly. Below 60%, cigars dry out, crack, and lose flavor. If you catch the problem before cracking appears, a gradual rehydration over two to four weeks typically restores smokability. The mistake most people make is rushing it: placing a very dry cigar back into 70% RH causes the outer wrapper to absorb moisture faster than the interior filler, which can cause splits at the seam. Start at 62, 65% for the first week, then slowly step up to your normal target. Our full rehydration guide covers this process in detail.

FAQs

What humidity should I target in a dry climate like Arizona or Colorado? Aim for 69, 72% RH inside your storage container. Dry ambient air pulls moisture out quickly, so starting at the high end of the safe range gives you a buffer against dips below 65%.

Why does my humidor lose humidity so fast in a desert climate? Wood is porous and breathes with its environment. When the air outside your humidor sits at 20, 30% RH, common in Phoenix or Albuquerque, it draws moisture out through the wood grain much faster than in humid regions.

Is a tupperdor better than a wood humidor in an arid climate? Yes, for most dry-climate collectors. Airtight plastic or acrylic containers lose far less moisture to ambient air than wood humidors, which means your humidity packs last longer and your RH stays more stable with less maintenance.

How often should I check my humidor in a dry climate? At minimum once a week, more often in winter when heating systems strip even more indoor humidity. Use a calibrated digital hygrometer and keep a log so you can catch trends before cigars are affected.

Can I use multiple Boveda packs to fight dry conditions? Yes. Using two or three packs rated at 69% or 72% in a larger humidor will help maintain RH more reliably, since each pack has a limited surface area. Do not mix packs of different RH ratings — they will fight each other and neither will hold its target correctly.
