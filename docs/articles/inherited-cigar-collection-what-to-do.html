Inherited a Cigar Collection? Here's What to Do

By Stick Picks · Published October 15, 2026

Quick Answer

If you've inherited a cigar collection, act fast: move everything to a stable, climate-controlled space, leave the humidification source in place, and don't remove cigars from their boxes. Then assess condition, identify what you have, get a valuation, and decide whether to smoke, gift, trade, or sell.

Inheriting a cigar collection puts you in genuinely unfamiliar territory, even if you’re an experienced collector yourself. The cigars may be decades old. The humidor might still be humming along, or it might have been sitting in a garage since last winter. Either way, the decisions you make in the first 48-72 hours matter more than almost anything that follows.

What should I do the moment I discover an inherited collection?

A question we hear often: what’s the single most important first move?

Don’t disturb it more than you have to. Keep everything somewhere climate-controlled and leave the humidification source in place. Don’t remove cigars from their boxes unless you have to: the box offers some protection, keeps things organized, and makes it easier for someone who knows the brands to quickly assess what’s there and what it might be worth.

The worst thing an inherited collection encounters is a well-meaning person who moves it into an attic, a basement with no climate control, or an unheated garage. Temperature swings are just as damaging as dry air. A cold garage in winter or a hot attic in summer will destroy a collection faster than outright neglect. If the humidor is still sealed and the humidification device still has moisture, you may have bought yourself days or even weeks to make thoughtful decisions.

Once you’ve secured the environment, your next job is a calm, methodical assessment. Not a smoke session.

How do I know if the cigars are still any good?

This one comes up a lot. The short answer: you have to look, feel, and eventually smell them.

You may find yourself suddenly responsible for preserving cigars that have been collecting dust for years, or dealing with a collection that hasn’t been properly humidified in some time. There’s also the task of identifying what you actually have, especially if the collection includes rare or potentially valuable sticks.

Run a physical check on a sample from each box:

The squeeze test. Gently roll the cigar between your fingertips. It should have a slight, even give: not rock-hard, not spongy. If they’re hard as a rock, you may be able to restore them to smokeable condition after careful time in a well-balanced humidor. If most of the oils have evaporated, they may not come back, but they’re worth a try.

Visual inspection. Look for cracking wrappers, white or green fuzz (mold), or tiny pinholes in the wrapper leaf (beetle damage). If you find mold or beetle damage, isolate those cigars immediately to keep it from spreading to the rest of the collection.

Aroma check. A good cigar should still carry some earthy tobacco scent even after years. An ammonia smell suggests the tobacco is still off-gassing, which is often fixable with rest. A musty or dusty smell may point to mold or poor storage.

If cigars are slightly dry but otherwise intact, our guide on how to rehydrate dry cigars walks through the slow, safe way to bring them back. Don’t rush this process.

How do I actually catalog and identify what I’ve inherited?

Readers frequently ask: “I have hundreds of cigars and I don’t recognize half the brands. Where do I even start?”

Organized documentation pays off immediately here. Use a notebook or spreadsheet to record the brand and specific name of each cigar, size (length and ring gauge), quantity, condition notes, and date of manufacture if known. Digital tools that scan cigar bands can automatically identify the cigar and pull up details on potential value.

For each cigar or box, photograph:

  1. The full cigar band (front and back if possible)
  2. The box end label, which often carries date codes and factory marks
  3. Any seals, certifications, or warranty stickers still intact on sealed boxes

A picture of a cigar with the band still on lets you reference it at any retail shop to track it down again. There’s nothing worse than remembering how good a cigar was, then chasing it like a needle in a haystack because you can’t recall what the band looked like.

What determines whether inherited cigars have real monetary value?

The honest answer: most cigars don’t appreciate in value, and only a small segment of a typical collection will be worth meaningful money on the secondary market.

The main factors are brand (Cohiba, Montecristo, and Partagás are generally the most sought-after), age (properly stored older cigars can become more valuable over time), condition (dry, damaged, or poorly stored cigars lose value significantly), packaging (sealed boxes and original presentation matter more than most people expect), and rarity (limited editions and discontinued lines command a premium).

The 2014 Cohiba Robustos Supremos launched at £35 per stick and are now nearly impossible to find, with prices around £750 per cigar today. That kind of appreciation is the exception, not the rule, but it’s exactly why a careful inventory before any disposal decision is worth the time.

According to secondary market data, a box of Opus X that sold originally in 1995 for $200 could sell weeks later for twice that much, and with decades of aging since then, exceptional provenance examples command multiples of that today.

Where can I sell cigars I don’t want to keep?

A question we hear often: “I don’t smoke cigars myself. What are my realistic options?”

You have several paths, and the right one depends on quantity, quality, and how quickly you need to move the collection.

Specialist auction houses. For aged Cubans or rare limited editions, a tobacco auction specialist is often your best route. Auction houses regularly help people value and sell cigar collections of all sizes, from single boxes to entire humidors, and specialists are often happy to provide free initial valuations.

Cigar-specific secondary market platforms. Sites like PuroTrader exist specifically to connect buyers and sellers of premium and vintage cigars, taking into account the investable value of aged and rare sticks.

Local brick-and-mortar shops. A reputable local cigar shop can often help: they may take cigars on consignment or point you toward buyers in the local collector community.

Collector forums and communities. Established forums allow private sales between verified members, though building a reputation as a seller takes time, and for common cigars you may only get a fraction of retail value.

One important caveat: selling tobacco on eBay violates their rules and listings will be removed. Don’t go that route. Stick to tobacco-specific platforms or local private sales.

Should I keep any of it, even if I’m not really a cigar person?

This question is worth sitting with. An inherited collection often contains cigars that meant something to someone: bought for a milestone, set aside for a special occasion, or collected with real intention over years. Some experienced collectors keep a short list of people who know cigars and know what to do inside their humidor, a simple arrangement that costs nothing but means the collection lands in caring hands.

If you enjoy the occasional cigar, keeping a handful of the better sticks to smoke in appreciation of the person who collected them is entirely reasonable. If you’re building your own collection, folding in a few well-aged sticks from someone else’s humidor can add depth you couldn’t create yourself in the same timeframe.

And if you’d rather the whole collection go to people who will genuinely appreciate it, that’s a good outcome too. Gifting to a close friend who smokes, donating to a cigar herf or club event, or selling to a collector who will keep aging them are all respectful ways to honor what someone spent years building.

Frequently asked questions

Are inherited cigars still smokeable? Possibly, but it depends entirely on how they were stored. Cigars kept in a properly humidified environment at 65-70% RH and 65-70°F can remain excellent for decades. Dry, cracked, or beetle-damaged cigars may be unsalvageable, but even slightly dry sticks can often be slowly rehydrated over weeks in a well-maintained humidor.

How do I find out what an inherited cigar collection is worth? Start by cataloging every box: brand, vitola, quantity, and condition. Then cross-reference current secondary-market prices on platforms like PuroTrader or auction houses that specialize in cigars. For rare or aged Cubans especially, a specialist retailer or auction house can offer a free valuation. Condition and original packaging dramatically affect value.

Can I sell inherited cigars legally? In the U.S., selling tobacco privately is legal in most states but regulated. You generally cannot sell cigars on platforms like eBay. Options include cigar-specific auction sites, consignment through a local brick-and-mortar shop, or private sales through collector forums. Always check your state’s tobacco regulations before listing.

What’s the first thing I should do with a humidor I’ve inherited? Don’t open it repeatedly or move it to an uncontrolled environment. Keep the humidification device in place, check that it still has moisture, and place the humidor somewhere with stable temperature, ideally 65-70°F. Rapid temperature or humidity swings are the fastest way to damage what could be a valuable collection.

How do I identify cigars in an inherited collection with no labels or notes? Start with the cigar band: most premium cigars have distinctive band art and text. Apps and tools that scan cigar bands (like Stick Picks) can identify cigars instantly. For loose cigars without bands, look at the box markings, date codes, and country of origin stamps. A knowledgeable retailer or collector community can help identify unusual or unlabeled sticks.
