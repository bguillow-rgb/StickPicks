A herf is one of the most enjoyable experiences in the cigar hobby, and one of the least explained. If you've spent any time on cigar forums or in a well-stocked lounge, you've almost certainly heard the word. Maybe someone invited you to one, or you saw a post announcing a "Saturday herf" and had no idea what you were agreeing to. This guide covers everything: what herfing actually is, where the word came from, how to attend one well, how to host one, and what the unwritten rules are that make the difference between a memorable afternoon and an awkward one.

---

## So what exactly is a herf, and why does it have such a strange name?

A herf is, technically, nothing more than a gathering of people for the purpose of smoking cigars. What sets it apart from an average cigar shop visit is that a herf usually takes place somewhere else: a cigar-friendly bar, a backyard, or someone's living room.

The name's origin is a small piece of cigar community history. The word "herf" first entered the cigar lexicon on November 21, 1996, on the alt.smokers.cigar (ASC) newsgroup, introduced in a casual posting. The online community picked it up fast and ran with it. Other than being coined during the mid-1990s cigar boom by that newsgroup, no one seems to know its deeper roots.

What started as internet slang has since become a fully established part of cigar culture vocabulary. Herf is a peculiar piece of language: it works as a noun, a verb, an adjective. You *attend* a herf, you *herf* with friends, and yes, someone is always "the herfing type." The word stuck because the concept it describes, intentional, relaxed, tobacco-centered fellowship, doesn't have a better English equivalent.

## What actually happens at a herf?

*A question we hear often:* People who've never attended one tend to imagine either something very formal (a cigar dinner with courses and speeches) or very chaotic. Reality sits comfortably between those extremes.

What happens at a herf varies from one event to the next. The host and attendees set the tone. The one guarantee is that the smoke flows. At most herfs, a good deal of trading and gifting takes place too.

Beyond the smoke, herfs tend to revolve around conversation: about the cigars themselves, sure, but also about whatever the attendees care about. There's no agenda, no formality, no obligation to work through a tasting rubric. If you want to take notes on what you're smoking, do it. If you just want to talk, do that instead.

A herf is, at its core, a cigar get-together where fellow smokers meet to relax and share. It can be held at a cigar store, a bar that permits cigar smoking, or at someone's home, usually outside in the backyard.

Trades and passes are a natural part of the rhythm. Someone lights something interesting, the person next to them asks about it, and suddenly half the table is cutting the same stick. This informal circulation is what gives herfs their distinct feel. Cigar collecting is normally a solitary hobby; a herf is where it becomes communal.

## What should I bring to my first herf?

*This one comes up a lot:* First-time attendees often overthink it.

The core answer is simple. Bring cigars you actually like, in enough quantity to share. A practical baseline is one cigar per person you expect to smoke with, plus two extras, one for trading and one buffer in case you want a second smoke or someone shows up unannounced. If the herf is small (four to six people), five or six sticks in varied strengths covers you well.

Vary the strength profile. A mild-to-medium Connecticut or Claro, a medium Habano-wrapped option, and one fuller-bodied stick gives the group something to choose from regardless of pace or preference. Samplers work well here because smoking occasions vary: someone might want a shorter weekday-style cigar, another a longer afternoon smoke. A mixed selection respects that.

Don't show up without a cutter and a reliable lighter. Borrowing tools all evening gets old for everyone. If you're new and still building your kit, a single-flame torch is compact and dependable. Beyond that: a small travel ashtray if you're not sure the venue has good ones, and a way to take notes (even just a notes app) if you want to remember what you smoked.

## What are the unwritten rules of herfing?

| Situation | Right move | Wrong move |
|---|---|---|
| Someone lights something you want to try | Ask politely; accept a no gracefully | Grab their cigar without asking |
| Trading | Offer freely, equal value isn't required | Pressure someone into swapping down |
| Your cigar goes out | Relight quietly without commentary | Make a production of it or blame the host |
| Someone else's cigar canoes or has draw issues | Ignore it unless they ask for help | Unsolicited criticism of their stick |
| A new person arrives mid-herf | Offer them a cigar if you have one to spare | Make them feel like they missed an entry window |
| You're over it | Leave quietly when ready | Announce your departure with a ten-minute speech |

The underlying principle is generosity without obligation. The cigar hobby attracts a genuinely friendly crowd, and herfs exist because enthusiasts enjoy sharing what they know and what they have. That spirit is fragile. The fastest way to kill it is to treat a trade like a negotiation or critique someone's taste in front of the group.

One more thing: pace yourself. Your palate fatigues faster than you'd expect, especially back-to-back. Three well-spaced sticks with time to talk beats six rushed ones every time.

## How do I host a herf at home?

*Readers frequently ask:* what does a host actually need to prepare?

Less than you think. The essentials are a smoking-friendly outdoor or well-ventilated space, something to drink (coffee, beer, and spirits all pair well with cigars, and espresso holds its own against almost any stick), a supply of ashtrays, and enough seating so nobody is standing awkwardly for two hours.

On music: keep it ambient and unobtrusive. The conversation is the point. Background jazz, low-key blues, or acoustic anything works. Anything that competes with conversation is a mistake.

You don't need to put together a tasting menu or a formal progression. That's a cigar dinner, a different and perfectly valid format. A herf is informal by design. The host's job is to create a space where people feel comfortable showing up with what they have and staying as long as they want. Invitations can go out a week ahead or a day ahead. The format is flexible.

If you want to sharpen the experience without overcomplicating it, consider putting out a small tasting card or whiteboard so guests can note what they're smoking. It's a light touch that gives the session a through-line and makes it easier to remember what you liked when you get home. ## Is there a difference between a herf and just smoking together?

Technically, no. In practice, the word carries an implied level of intentionality. When someone calls it a herf, it means people showed up specifically to smoke together, not just happened to light up in the same place.

Cigar culture has resurged over the past few years, pulling in a wave of newer, younger collectors who discovered the hobby through friends, social media, or a single memorable smoke at a celebration. For many of them, that first purchase turns into a second, then a small collection, then a genuine hobby. Herfs are often the social infrastructure that turns isolated collectors into a community. The cigar world's reputation for friendliness isn't accidental. It's maintained event by event, trade by trade.

## FAQs

**Where did the word 'herf' come from?**

The word first entered the cigar community lexicon on November 21, 1996, on the alt.smokers.cigar (ASC) newsgroup, introduced in a casual posting. The community picked it up quickly and it spread from there.

**How many cigars should I bring to a herf?**

Bring one per expected attendee plus two extras for trades or courtesy offers. A mix of mild-to-medium and one bolder stick is a versatile starting spread.

**Is it rude to show up with budget cigars?**

Not at all. The cigar hobby attracts a genuinely friendly crowd. Occasional snobs exist, as in any hobby, but they're the exception. Bring what you enjoy and are proud to share.

**What's the trading etiquette?**

Offer freely, accept a no without explanation, and never pressure someone to trade down. The spirit is generosity, not deal-making. Even trades are nice but never required.
