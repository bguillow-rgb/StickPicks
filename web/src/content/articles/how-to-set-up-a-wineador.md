A wineador is the natural next step when your desktop humidor starts running out of room and you're not ready to build a full walk-in cedar cabinet. It's a thermoelectric wine cooler converted into a climate-controlled cigar storage unit, capable of housing anywhere from a few hundred to well over 500 sticks. Done right, it offers something a wooden tabletop box simply cannot: tight temperature control in a large enclosure, with enough space to think in boxes rather than individual cigars.

This guide walks through everything you need to get a wineador dialed in, from choosing the right cooler to humidification options, cedar setup, and common first-timer mistakes worth skipping.

---

## Why would I want a wineador instead of just buying a bigger humidor?

*A question we hear often:*

The short answer is temperature control. A traditional wooden humidor manages humidity but has no mechanism to regulate temperature beyond whatever room it lives in. If your home gets warm in summer, your cigars do too, and proper aging comes down to keeping both temperature and humidity stable. Extra heat from the environment will hurt, not help.

The main reason people choose wineadors is those temperature controls, which matter most if you live in a hot climate. Most collectors aren't willing to keep their entire home at or below 70°F purely for the sake of their cigars. A wineador solves that by maintaining a sealed, cooled environment regardless of what's happening outside it.

Capacity is the other driver. A standard 25 or 50-count humidor can't house all your cigars once you've accumulated five or six boxes, and that's when people get creative about storage. A properly configured wineador closes that gap without requiring carpentry skills or a dedicated room.

## What kind of wine cooler should I actually buy?

*This one comes up a lot:*

Not all wine fridges convert equally. The most important specification is cooling technology: always choose a thermoelectric cooler, not a compressor model. Thermoelectric units don't have a traditional condenser, so they don't produce the aggressive humidity swings that compressor units do when they cycle on and off. That cycling works directly against stable cigar storage.

Look for a model with a glass door, a solid magnetic door seal, and adjustable interior shelving. The airtight refrigeration seals common to wine coolers are genuinely useful here, and any built-in climate control technology makes it easier to hold a consistent temperature without constant fiddling.

If budget is tight, a basic thermoelectric cooler from a hardware store or secondhand marketplace works fine. You'll add your own cedar later. If you want something closer to plug-and-play, dedicated cigar wineadors come pre-fitted with cedar shelving. NewAir cigar cabinets, for example, hold between 250 and 840 cigars and come outfitted with cedar shelves and a heating and cooling system designed to hold consistent conditions. Factory-ready units run roughly $400-$1,500 depending on capacity.

## Step-by-step: how do I actually set one up?

Here's the complete setup sequence, in order:

**1. Plug the drain hole.**

Most wineadors have a drain hole in the bottom, there to let excess condensation escape when the unit is used for wine. That same hole bleeds humidity. Seal it with a rubber stopper, aquarium-safe silicone, or cork before doing anything else.

**2. Check the door seal.**

The door seal is the next most common place to lose humidity. Grab a flashlight, close the door, shine it through the glass toward the seal, and look for any light escaping around the perimeter. A gap isn't unusual on older or budget units. A thin strip of weatherstripping fixes it cheaply.

**3. Clean the interior.**

Wipe down the inside with distilled water, or a light mixture of distilled water and isopropyl alcohol. New units sometimes smell of plastic. If that's the case, leave the door open and the unit off for a few days, or set a dish of baking soda inside to pull the odor out.

**4. Install Spanish cedar.**

Most wine coolers come with wire racks sized for bottles, which aren't ideal for cigars. Cedar is what you want: it reacts well with moisture and repels cigar beetles. You can buy pre-made cedar drawers from specialty vendors, line existing wire racks with cedar trays, or set old cigar boxes directly on the racks as a stopgap while you wait for custom drawers.

**5. Season the cedar.**

The wineador body doesn't need seasoning, but the cedar does. Lightly wipe all cedar surfaces with distilled water on a lint-free cloth, place an open container of distilled water inside, close the unit, and let it sit for 48-72 hours before adding humidification media.

**6. Add humidification and a hygrometer, then stabilize.**

Set your target temperature to 65-70°F. Add your humidification source (covered below), place at least one calibrated hygrometer inside, and let everything settle for 48-72 hours before loading a single cigar. You're looking to establish equilibrium around 70% humidity and 70°F before the cigars go in.

## What's the best humidification method for a wineador?

Two options handle the majority of wineadors in real-world use.

**Boveda 320g packs** are the easiest, most hands-off choice. They provide two-way humidity control, pulling moisture in or releasing it depending on conditions. Mounting brackets are available if you want them fixed in place rather than sitting loose. The tradeoff is cost: a large wineador may need four to six packs at once, and they need periodic replacement when depleted.

**Unscented silica-based kitty litter** is the budget alternative. It's cheap and holds a significant amount of moisture, but it requires regular attention: adding distilled water, checking levels, and dialing in how much you need for your specific unit. Many experienced collectors run a hybrid, using silica beads as the base humidity reservoir with a few Boveda packs distributed through the drawers for fine-tuning.

For hygrometers, use at least two: one near the top, one near the bottom, positioned so you can read them without opening the door. Humidity stratifies in a large enclosure, so monitoring both zones catches problems before they affect your cigars.

## Wineador vs. Coolidor vs. Traditional Humidor: which is right for me?

| Feature | Desktop Humidor | Coolidor | Wineador |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature control | None | None | Active cooling |
| Typical capacity | 25-150 cigars | 100-300 cigars | 250-600+ cigars |
| Setup cost | $50-$300 | $30-$80 (DIY) | $150-$1,500 |
| Humidity control | Manual/Boveda | Boveda/beads | Boveda/beads |
| Best for | Everyday rotation | Budget bulk storage | Serious aging collections |
| Beetle risk mitigation | Passive | Passive | Active (temp control) |
| Aesthetic | High | Low | Medium-High |

A coolidor (a converted plastic cooler) is the cheapest way to scale up storage but offers no temperature control. If you live somewhere climate-controlled that never exceeds 70°F, a coolidor works fine. A wineador earns its higher price point for collectors in warm climates, anyone aging cigars long-term, or anyone who wants the certainty that beetles simply cannot activate at 68°F.

## How do I arrange cigars inside once it's ready?

*Readers frequently ask:*

The most practical arrangement depends on whether you collect primarily singles or full boxes. Full-box collectors do well with wire racks lined in cedar: stack boxes directly, label them facing outward, and you can navigate the whole collection visually. Singles and five-packs are better suited to cedar drawers, which keep sticks organized by blend or region without stacking.

A common real-world setup is a mix: two or three shelves for box storage near the top, two or three drawers for singles near the bottom. One approach is to start with four double drawers and two shelves, then modify the shelves with cedar edging over time to create additional single-cigar storage as the collection grows.

Don't pack it to capacity right away. Airflow between cigars matters. Leaving some room around each row helps humidity distribute evenly and makes rotation far less of a project. Once your wineador holds 200-plus cigars, keeping track of what's in there without opening the door becomes its own problem. ## What mistakes do first-time wineador owners make?

Five to avoid immediately:

1. **Using a compressor cooler.** The humidity swings caused by compressor cycling are brutal for tobacco. Thermoelectric only.
2. **Skipping the drain plug.** This is the single most common humidity leak in DIY builds.
3. **Loading cigars before stabilization.** Two days of patience prevents weeks of humidity chasing.
4. **Letting sunlight hit the glass door.** Direct sunlight will push both temperature and humidity off target inside the unit.
5. **Setting temperature too high.** Keep the cooler below 72°F at all times. Cigar beetle larvae can activate above that threshold, and active temperature control is the main advantage a wineador has over a standard humidor.

## FAQs

**What is a wineador?**
A wineador is a wine cooler, typically thermoelectric, converted into a climate-controlled cigar humidor, usually by adding Spanish cedar shelving or drawers and a dedicated humidification system.

**How many cigars can a wineador hold?**
It depends on the unit size. A typical 28-bottle thermoelectric cooler converted with cedar drawers can hold 400-600 cigars. Dedicated cigar wineadors are factory-rated from 250 to 840 cigars depending on the model.

**Do I need to season a wineador?**
The cooler body itself does not need seasoning, but any Spanish cedar shelves, drawers, or trays installed inside must be seasoned before loading cigars, the same process used for a traditional humidor.

**Should I use Boveda packs or silica beads in a wineador?**
Both work well. Boveda 320g packs are the most hands-off option. Unscented silica-based kitty litter or silica beads are more economical for large units but require periodic recharging with distilled water. Many collectors use a hybrid of both.

**Can I store wine and cigars together in a wineador?**
Only as a short-term measure, a few days at most. Wine bottles can transfer odors to tobacco, and the ideal storage conditions for each don't align well. Dedicated cigar-only use is strongly recommended.

**What temperature should a wineador be set to for cigars?**
Target 65-70°F (18-21°C), paired with 65-70% relative humidity. Staying below 72°F matters most for beetle prevention, which is the primary advantage of active cooling over passive humidor storage.
