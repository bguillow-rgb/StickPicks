Whether you've just picked up your first box of robustos or you're a seasoned collector with a humidor that needs its own zip code, *how* you light your cigar matters almost as much as what you're smoking. The wrong tool, or the right tool used badly, can torch your wrapper, produce an uneven burn, or coat a $30 stick in petroleum fumes before you take a single puff.

Here's exactly what you need to know.

## What are my actual options for lighting a cigar?

You have four meaningful choices: a butane torch lighter, a butane soft-flame lighter, long wooden matches, and cedar spills. A fifth option exists (fluid lighters like the classic Zippo) but experienced collectors almost universally reject it, for reasons we'll get into below.

Each option has a genuine use case. None of them is universally "correct." The best answer depends on where you're smoking, what size cigar you're lighting, and how much patience you brought to the occasion.

---

## Torch lighter vs. soft flame, what's the actual difference?

*A question we hear often:* people assume one is just "fancier" than the other. The real difference is physics.

Torch lighters burn at roughly 2,300 degrees Fahrenheit, about twice the temperature of a soft flame. That heat comes from pressurized butane forced through a narrow jet, producing the intensely blue, nearly invisible flame you see on torch lighters. They're also wind-resistant, which matters for outdoor smoking, and they light a cigar more evenly, which cuts down on canoeing.

Soft-flame butane lighters generate a gentle yellow flame similar to a match, at a much cooler temperature. They're more forgiving. Hold the flame a little too close or a little too long and you usually get away with it. That's a real advantage when you're still developing your lighting technique.

The downside of soft flame is time. On a thicker cigar, you're often looking at 20-40 seconds of careful toasting to fully char the foot, and any real wind will put you in a bad spot.

Neither flame type is inherently superior. Torch gets you speed and outdoor reliability. Soft flame gives you gentleness and forgiveness indoors.

---

## How many jets or flames do I actually need?

*This one comes up a lot:* new collectors often assume more jets means more prestige. In practice, it's about ring gauge and setting.

A single jet works fine for a 42-50 ring gauge cigar and is plenty for indoor use. Most collectors land on double-jet for everyday use: balanced, versatile, strong enough for robustos and toros without being too aggressive for a lounge. A triple flame makes quicker work of larger formats (54-60 ring gauge) but demands more distance to avoid overheating the tobacco.

Quad-jet lighters are mostly situational. Useful for a 60+ ring gauge behemoth or blustery outdoor conditions, but not something you need in daily rotation. For most people, double-jet is the workhorse.

## Can I just use matches? What about cedar spills?

Yes, and in many ways matches and cedar spills represent the purist ideal.

Long wooden matches (the 4-6 inch "fireplace" variety) burn cleanly and add no petroleum flavor. The technique is similar to a soft-flame lighter: toast the foot in a slow circle before drawing. One thing people skip is letting the sulfur burn off the tip for two or three seconds before bringing the flame near the cigar. Don't skip it. That initial burst of sulfur will absolutely taint your first puffs.

Cedar spills are thin strips of Spanish cedar, the same wood lining most quality humidors. Long before butane existed, cigars were lit this way. Cedar adds a faint, pleasant woodsy note that works with the tobacco rather than against it. The practical trade-off is real, though: you need a secondary flame source to light the spill, they're awkward outdoors, and they require enough patience to let the spill establish a proper ember before you approach the cigar's foot. For a quiet evening at home with a special stick, few methods feel as deliberate. For anything else, the hassle adds up fast.

---

## Why can't I use my Zippo?

*Readers frequently ask:* "I have a beautiful vintage Zippo, can I use it for my cigars?"

The problem is naphtha, the petroleum-based fuel that makes a Zippo so reliable in wind. That same fuel produces a hydrocarbon odor that porous tobacco leaf readily absorbs. You'll taste it, particularly on the first third of the cigar. Butane burns clean and leaves the tobacco's natural flavor alone. Naphtha doesn't.

Butane inserts exist that convert Zippos to a cleaner fuel source, which is a reasonable middle ground if you love the form factor. But if you've carefully stored and aged a cigar, starting the smoke with a fuel that introduces off-flavors is a poor trade.

## Does butane quality actually matter?

*A question we hear often:* "Butane is butane, right?"

It isn't. Cheap butane contains impurities that will eventually clog your lighter's jets and, more importantly, leave an unpleasant odor on your cigar. Given enough time, a jet that's clogged badly enough will stop firing reliably or stop firing at all.

Look for "triple-refined" or "5X refined" butane from reputable brands. The price premium over bargain butane is minimal, typically a dollar or two per canister. That's a cheap way to protect both your lighter and every cigar you light with it.

## How do I actually use a torch lighter correctly without scorching my cigar?

The most common mistake with a torch lighter is holding the flame too close to the foot for too long. Because the hottest part of the flame is nearly invisible, it's easy to misjudge the distance and incinerate the wrapper before you realize what's happening.

Hold the torch at roughly a 45-degree angle, keeping the tip of the visible blue cone about half an inch from the foot. Rotate the cigar slowly rather than charring one spot. Toast the outer ring of the foot first, working inward in a circle. Once the foot glows, take two or three light draws, then blow back gently into the cigar to check the burn. If one side is running ahead of the other, apply the jet at a distance to even it out.

With a soft-flame lighter, the same toasting approach applies, just slower and with the flame a bit closer. The lower temperature gives you more time to get the foot evenly lit before committing to that first draw.

## What about maintenance, do lighters need care?

They do, and neglect is how most lighters fail early. If you carry one in a pocket, lint and debris will find their way into the jets. Blow them out occasionally with compressed air.

Beyond that: purge a torch lighter before refilling by pressing the fill valve until you hear no more gas escaping. After refilling, let it sit for 30 seconds before igniting so the butane can stabilize at room temperature. Store it away from heat. A well-maintained lighter should last years without drama.

When components like ignition switches wear out, some torches can be sent in for repair. Worth knowing before you toss one.

## Travel tip: what can I bring on a plane?

Torch lighters are prohibited under TSA rules. Don't pack your favorite torch in your pocket, carry-on, or checked luggage. It will be confiscated. A single soft-flame butane lighter is permitted on your person or in a carry-on. Cedar spills and long matches are generally fine in checked baggage, though you should verify current airline rules before flying. ## FAQs

**Can I use a Zippo to light a cigar?**
Technically yes, but most experienced smokers avoid it. Zippos burn liquid naphtha fuel, which carries a strong petroleum odor that seeps into the tobacco and ruins the flavor of a premium cigar. Stick to butane lighters or cedar spills.

**How many flames does a cigar lighter need?**
It depends on ring gauge. A single-jet torch works well for smaller cigars (42-50 ring gauge). Double-jet is the most versatile everyday choice. Triple or quad jets speed up lighting on 54+ ring gauge cigars but require more distance to avoid scorching the wrapper.

**Are cedar spills better than a lighter for cigars?**
Many purists say yes -- cedar spills impart no foreign flavor and add a faint pleasant aroma to the light. The trade-off is that they require more technique and patience and are impractical outdoors or on the go.

**What butane quality should I use to refill a cigar lighter?**
Always use triple-refined or "zero-impurity" butane. Cheap butane contains contaminants that clog lighter jets over time and can leave an off-flavor on your cigar. Quality butane costs a bit more but protects both the lighter and the smoke.

**Can I bring a torch lighter on a plane?**
No. Torch lighters are prohibited in carry-on bags, checked luggage, and on your person under TSA and IATA rules. A single soft-flame butane lighter is permitted in a carry-on or on your person. Check our [travel guide](/how-to-travel-with-cigars) for full packing tips.
