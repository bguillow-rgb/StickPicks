A **figurado** is the cigar world's umbrella term for any shape that breaks from the standard straight cylinder. If a cigar narrows at the head, widens through the middle, tapers at both ends, or spirals into something almost sculptural, it's a figurado. Everything else, the Robustos, Toros, Coronas, and Churchills you reach for most often, belongs to the parejo family. Knowing what makes a figurado distinct changes how you cut it, light it, and even where you place it in your humidor.

---

## "I keep seeing the word figurado, what does it actually mean?"

At its simplest: a parejo has straight sides, an open foot, and a rounded capped head. A figurado breaks from that basic cylinder with a tapered head, tapered foot, wider middle, or some other deliberate contour.

The word comes from Spanish, *figura*, meaning shape or figure, and it works as a category label rather than a single size. Torpedo, pyramid, perfecto, salomon, and culebra are all figurados. There is no single figurado vitola the way there is a single Robusto; figurado just tells you the cigar isn't straight.

Figurados take more rolling skill because the bunch, binder, and wrapper must follow a changing diameter without creating tight spots or loose sections. The shape can also change airflow and the proportions of burning tobacco as the cigar progresses. That's part of what makes them worth collecting: the smoke genuinely evolves from tip to foot in ways a parejo often doesn't.

---

## "What are all the different figurado shapes? I can never keep them straight."

*A question we hear often:* collectors encounter these names constantly but rarely see them all compared side by side. Here's a practical breakdown:

| Shape | Defining Feature | Typical Size | Cut Advice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Torpedo | Straight sides, long pointed head | 6-7 in x 50-52 rg | Straight cut, 1-2 mm from tip |
| Belicoso | Shorter, gentle taper at head | 5-5.5 in x 50+ rg | Small straight cut or scissors |
| Pyramid | Wide open foot, narrows to a point at head | 6-7 in x 52-54 rg | Very conservative cut at tip |
| Perfecto | Tapers at both ends, wider middle | 4.5-6 in x varies | Toast foot gently; cut head conservatively |
| Salomon | Long, large-ring figurado similar to a perfecto | 7+ in x 57-60 rg | Scissors or straight cutter |
| Culebra | Three intertwined parejos twisted together | ~5.5 in x 38 rg | Unwind before smoking; cut each individually |

A perfecto is wider in the middle and tapers at both ends. The torpedo is more common: straight sides, but with a long tapered head. The pyramid gets confused with the torpedo constantly. The key difference is the foot: a pyramid's foot is wide open and flat, while a torpedo's foot is the same gauge as its body.

The culebra is three intertwined parejo sticks, smoked together or separated. It's a conversation piece as much as a smoke, and genuinely rare in modern production.

## "Do figurados actually taste different, or is that just marketing?"

*This one comes up a lot:* skeptics wonder if the shape is pure theater. It isn't, though the effect is subtler than some retailers suggest.

Figurados can alter airflow through the cigar in ways that accentuate certain flavors in the tobacco's profile. A tapered head concentrates smoke through a smaller opening in the first third, which tends to produce a more focused, sometimes more intense flavor in the early puffs. As the ring gauge opens through the body, the draw loosens and the flavor broadens. You're tasting the same blend at two or three different burn widths in a single cigar.

This is most pronounced in a perfecto or salomon, where the diameter change is dramatic. In a torpedo, the effect is more modest because only the head tapers significantly. Whether that evolution justifies seeking figurados over parejos is personal, but it's not imaginary. A lot of experienced collectors keep a few figurados on hand specifically when they want a more dynamic smoke rather than a consistent, steady experience from first light to final third.

Only the most skilled torcedors can roll figurados, especially the more complex ones. That smaller pool of rollers means smaller batches than your standard parejos. Combined with higher labor costs, figurados from reputable factories tend to carry a modest price premium, typically $3-8 more per stick over the same factory's equivalent parejo blend, according to general industry pricing across major retailers.

## "How do I cut and light a figurado without ruining it?"

*Readers frequently ask:* the cut is where most figurado mistakes happen.

The rule is conservative: remove less than you think you need, test the draw, then take a second sliver if necessary. A narrow foot may need a more controlled light. For a pyramid or sharp torpedo, a straight cutter or cigar scissors gives you the most control. A punch cut risks cracking a pointed head rather than cleanly removing it. A V-cut works on a torpedo with a modest taper but can collapse on a very sharp point.

For the foot: most figurados have a standard open foot and light exactly like a parejo. Toast the foot in a slow circle until the edge glows evenly before taking your first draw. The exception is the perfecto, which often has a nearly closed or pinched foot. Toast that pinched tip slowly until it opens before drawing; rushing the light can crack the wrapper at the narrow point before the tobacco is properly burning.

Once lit, pacing is identical to any premium cigar. One relaxed draw every 45-60 seconds keeps the burn temperature down and the flavor clean.

## "How should I store figurados in my humidor? Do they need special treatment?"

The tobacco inside a figurado ages and stores under the same principles as any premium cigar: 65-70% relative humidity, 65-70 degrees Fahrenheit, away from light and vibration. What changes is the physical handling.

A pointed or tapered head is structurally more fragile than a rounded cap. If you stack figurados horizontally the way you might stack Robustos, the tips at the bottom can crack under the weight of cigars above them. The fix is simple: store figurados with their heads pointing up when possible, and if you're stacking in a tray, use a single layer rather than a deep stack. Tubes or individual cedar sleeves offer extra protection for particularly valuable or delicate perfectos.

The [cigar sick period after shipping](https://stickpicks.app/articles/cigar-sick-period-after-shipping) applies equally. Give any figurado at least two to three weeks of rest in a properly humidified environment after it arrives before smoking it, regardless of shape. And if you're [aging figurados long-term](https://stickpicks.app/articles/how-long-to-age-cigars), tracking them by shape and purchase date matters: a perfecto from the same blend may smoke noticeably differently from a parejo after three years because the proportion of ligero-heavy core tobacco to the overall draw changes as humidity works through the varying diameters. ## "Are figurados worth collecting, or should I stick to parejos?"

*A question we hear often:* the honest answer is both, intentionally.

Unlike a parejo, a figurado is not uniformly straight from head to foot. It takes more time and considerably more skill to make. A figurado can also deliver a wholly different smoke from a parejo of the same blend. That last point is the collector's reason to keep both: buy a torpedo and a robusto of the same blend, store them together, and you have a built-in tasting experiment. Smoke the robusto at one year; smoke the torpedo at three. The differences in flavor evolution, not just from aging but from the shape's influence on the burn, make for genuinely interesting side-by-side notes.

From a value perspective, limited-edition figurados, particularly perfectos and salomons from established Nicaraguan and Dominican factories, have historically held secondary-market value better than equivalent parejos of the same release, partly because their smaller production runs make them scarcer. That doesn't mean you should buy figurados purely as investments, but if you're already choosing between two expressions of a blend you love, the figurado is often the more interesting long-term cellar choice.

If you're just starting to explore shaped cigars, torpedos are the most forgiving entry point. The taper is modest, the cut is straightforward, and the flavor transition is noticeable without being jarring.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a figurado and a parejo cigar?**
A parejo has straight, parallel sides from foot to cap, think Robusto or Toro. A figurado departs from that cylinder with a tapered head, tapered foot, bulging middle, or some combination. Every torpedo, pyramid, perfecto, salomon, and culebra is a figurado; most other cigars are parejos.

**Are figurado cigars harder to smoke than parejos?**
Not harder, but slightly different. A tapered head concentrates flavor at the tip and opens gradually as you smoke. Cut too little and the draw is tight; cut too much and you lose the taper's benefit. Pacing is the same, one draw every 45-60 seconds, but you may notice more pronounced flavor transitions as the ring gauge widens.

**How do you cut a figurado cigar?**
Start with a conservative cut, remove just 1-2 mm from the tapered tip. Test the draw; if it's too tight, take a second small slice. A straight cutter or scissors works well. Avoid a punch cut, which can crack a pointed head. A V-cut is viable on a torpedo but less ideal on a sharp pyramid tip.

**Do figurado cigars age differently than parejos?**
The tobacco inside ages the same way, fermentation continues, harshness mellows, and complexity develops regardless of shape. The main storage difference is physical: a pointed head is more fragile than a rounded cap, so store figurados tip-side up and in a single layer where possible to avoid cracking the wrapper at the taper.

**What is a perfecto cigar?**
A perfecto is a double figurado, it tapers at both the head and the foot, with a wider, sometimes bulbous midsection. The closed or nearly closed foot means you must toast the tip carefully before it opens into a full draw. Perfectos are often shorter smokes (4.5-6 inches) but are considered among the most demanding shapes to roll well.
