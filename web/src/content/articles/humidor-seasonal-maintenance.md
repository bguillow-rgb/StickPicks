Your humidor doesn't live in a climate-controlled vacuum. It lives in your home, your office, or your garage, and it feels every season change your house does. Winter heating strips moisture from the air; summer heat and humidity flood it back in. If you're not compensating for that, your cigars are quietly paying the price through cracked wrappers in January and mold-friendly spikes in July.

Here's how to stay ahead of the calendar.

## Why does my humidor humidity drop so much in winter?

*A question we hear often:* collectors are mystified when their RH reads a rock-solid 68% all autumn, then crashes to 58, 61% the moment they flip on the furnace.

Summer might be oppressively humid, but winter air holds far less moisture, and that imbalance shows up directly in your RH readings. Your heating system makes it worse: you turn up the heat because it's cold, the air inside gets drier, and your humidor drops a few points as a result.

What you're really dealing with is two opponents at once. Thirsty dry air leaks around your lid, and the Spanish cedar lining absorbs moisture too. If the air surrounding a wooden humidor is incredibly dry, that air draws moisture directly from the wood. That double demand empties your humidifier faster than you'd expect, sometimes within days rather than weeks.

What to do:
- Check your humidor weekly in January and February, not monthly
- Refill or replace your humidification device before it runs completely dry
- Consider stepping up to a higher-RH Boveda pack (69% instead of 65%, for instance) for the winter months
- If you use a gel or foam humidifier, increase refill frequency or add a supplemental pack

---

## How bad is a 5, 7 point RH swing, really?

This one comes up a lot, especially from collectors who figure a dip from 68% to 62% is close enough.

It isn't. A humidor that reads 68% on average but swings between 62% and 74% is more damaging than one that holds a steady 72%. Tobacco leaves are hygroscopic: they expand and contract with moisture changes. Rapid swings crack wrappers, loosen the binder, and cause uneven aging across a single cigar. Changes in conditions cause your cigars to expand and contract, and that has a real impact on flavor, construction, and overall integrity.

One veteran cigar advisor put it plainly: "I've seen cigars crack within a few hours of being exposed to extremely dry air." That's an extreme case, but it shows how responsive tobacco is to ambient conditions.

The practical point: consistency beats any specific number. A stable 65% is far better for your collection than one that averages 70% but swings wildly. This is exactly why tracking your readings matters. Apps like [Stick Picks](https://stickpicks.app) let you log condition notes alongside each cigar, so you can spot a pattern if a cigar starts smoking rough after a cold snap.

## Why does my humidor run too wet in summer?

*Readers frequently ask:* what's happening when the hygrometer reads 75% or higher in July after holding steady all spring?

External temperature shifts affect evaporation and condensation directly. In warmer climates or during summer months, humidity tends to rise, and the humidor needs more attention as a result.

The specific danger goes beyond cigars feeling soft and spongy, though they will. Sustained RH above 72% creates the conditions cigar beetles need to hatch. According to entomological research cited by storage specialists, tobacco beetle eggs hatch most readily above 72% RH and 72°F simultaneously, which is exactly where an unmanaged summer humidor can drift.

One-way humidifiers make this worse. Propylene glycol solutions and water reservoirs don't know when to stop. They keep adding moisture at night when the temperature drops, and they keep adding it through a rainy stretch too.

What to do in summer:
- Switch to a two-way humidity control system (like Boveda) that absorbs as well as emits
- Open your humidor briefly once a week to allow air exchange
- Store the humidor away from exterior walls and windows, where heat radiates most
- If you live in a consistently hot, humid climate, strongly consider a wineador or coolidor with active temperature control

## What's the right seasonal maintenance schedule?

| Season | Primary risk | Key action | Check frequency |
|--------|-------------|------------|-----------------|
| Winter | RH drops from heating | Refill humidifier more often; step up to higher RH pack | Weekly |
| Spring | Transition, can swing either way | Re-calibrate hygrometer; inspect seal | Every 2 weeks |
| Summer | RH spikes; beetle risk rises | Switch to 2-way control; monitor temps | Weekly |
| Fall | Transition, drying begins | Reseal if needed; re-season if it got dry | Every 2 weeks |

Run the dollar-bill test at every season change. At minimum, do it at the two that stress the seal most: the start of summer and the start of winter.

## How do I adjust for my specific climate, not just the season?

*This one comes up a lot:* collectors in Phoenix have a completely different problem than collectors in Miami, and generic seasonal advice doesn't always map to their reality.

Where you live shapes everything. Temperature, air conditioning, seasonal swings, and the baseline humidity of your region all affect how a humidor behaves, which means you have to adapt your maintenance to your local conditions, not just the month.

Here's a climate-based framework:

Dry climates (Southwest US, Mountain West, arid Europe): your humidor works harder year-round, not just in winter. In a hot, dry region where air conditioning runs constantly, humidity inside the humidor can drop quickly. AC removes moisture from the air while cooling the room, so you're fighting on two fronts even in summer. Use higher-capacity humidification (more Boveda packs, a larger bead jar) and check more often. A Spanish cedar tray insert can increase the wood's buffering capacity.

Humid climates (Southeast US, Gulf Coast, tropical regions): over-humidification in summer and the risk of mold are your main concerns. Run your humidor at 65, 68% rather than 70, 72% to give yourself a buffer. Keep a closer eye on the physical condition of your cigars. Any white bloom that doesn't wipe off cleanly needs immediate attention (see our guide to [cigar mold vs. plume](https://stickpicks.app/articles/cigar-mold-vs-plume)).

Variable climates (Midwest, Northeast US, Northern Europe): you'll face both problems depending on the month. In Minnesota, for example, collectors deal with desert-dry winters and muggy summers. Two-way humidity control packs are especially useful here because they handle both directions automatically.

## Does temperature matter as much as RH, or is it secondary?

Temperature and RH are linked. You can't fully manage one without understanding the other. Higher temps mean a lower RH is acceptable; cooler temps mean you can tolerate a bit more moisture. It's a balancing act.

For everyday smoking storage, your cigars will smoke well as long as you maintain a steady environment between 63, 70 degrees and 65, 72% RH. For long-term aging, cooler is better. Habanos S.A. recommends 65, 70% RH at 16, 18°C (61, 64°F) for archival aging, which is cooler than most room temperatures.

Heat also raises beetle hatch risk. If your storage area consistently exceeds 72°F in summer, that's a real argument for moving to an active cooling setup, even if your RH is technically in range.

Don't store your humidor near a heat vent, on a fireplace mantel, or in direct sunlight. A cool, consistent room is what you want.

## What if I let my humidor get too dry over winter, can I recover it?

Yes, and controlled re-seasoning is the right move. If the humidor and cigars are both competing for moisture, they'll deplete the humidification source faster. If your humidor has dried out from sitting unused, treat it like a new box and re-season it.

For a humidor that dried out with cigars inside, take a conservative approach. Don't flood it with 84% seasoning packs, which can shock dry cigars and crack their wrappers. Start with a 65% Boveda pack, let the system equilibrate for 5, 7 days, and bring the RH up gradually. Our full guide to [rehydrating dry cigars](https://stickpicks.app/articles/how-to-rehydrate-dry-cigars) covers the step-by-step process in detail.

If your cigars are still in decent shape (no cracking, some springiness remaining), they'll recover. If the wrapper is cracked through, no amount of humidity will undo structural damage. Smoke those now rather than storing them further.

## FAQs

**Why does my humidor lose humidity in winter?**
Indoor heating dries the air dramatically, pulling moisture out of your humidor faster than your humidifier can replace it. Cold air holds less moisture than warm air, so the ambient RH inside your home drops when the heat is on, and your humidor reacts accordingly.

**Why is my humidor too humid in summer?**
Warm, moist summer air leaks into your humidor, especially if the seal is imperfect. High ambient humidity combined with a one-way humidifier that keeps emitting moisture can push your RH above 72%, creating ideal conditions for mold and cigar beetles.

**What RH should I target year-round?**
Most collectors aim for 65, 72% RH year-round. For long-term aging, the lower end of that range (65, 68%) is safer and reduces beetle risk. Consistency matters more than hitting an exact number. A stable 67% beats one that swings between 62% and 74%.

**How do I know if my humidor seal is failing seasonally?**
Run the dollar-bill test: close a dollar bill in the lid and pull it out. Resistance means a good seal; if it slides free easily, the seal is compromised. Do this at least twice a year, at the start of summer and winter, when ambient conditions stress the seal most.

**Should I use different Boveda pack strengths in summer vs. winter?**
Possibly. In a very dry winter climate, some collectors step up from a 65% to a 69% pack to compensate for the extra moisture demand. In a humid summer climate with a leaky lid, stepping down to a 62% or 65% pack can prevent over-humidification. Let your hygrometer guide the decision.
