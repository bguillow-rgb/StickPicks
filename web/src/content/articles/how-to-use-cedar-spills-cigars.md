A cedar spill is a thin strip of Spanish cedar that you light and use as a torch-free, chemical-free flame to toast and light a cigar. Instead of putting butane or match sulfur anywhere near a premium wrapper, you let a slow-burning sliver of the same wood that lines your humidor do the work. The result is a cleaner light, a faint cedar note on the first few puffs, and a deliberately unhurried ritual that suits the pace of a good cigar.

## What exactly is a cedar spill, and why would I bother?

A cedar spill is a strip of Spanish cedar lit from a candle, match, or lighter and then used to slowly toast the foot of a cigar. The spill itself never touches the cigar. It's an intermediary flame, the same principle as lighting a candle from another candle rather than a gas burner.

The practical case for bothering: burning cedar imparts a slight cedar flavor to the tobacco, and because spills are longer than a match, you have more time to properly light the foot. Butane torches are fast and windproof, but they run extremely hot. Surface temperatures near a torch nozzle can exceed 2,500°F, meaning any slight misjudgment scorches the wrapper before an even ember forms. A spill's lower, softer flame is far more forgiving for slow, deliberate toasting.

For collectors who've spent $30 or more on a single stick, that patience is worth developing.

## Where do cedar spills actually come from?

*This one comes up a lot:* people see cedar sheets inside factory cigar boxes and assume they're packing filler. They're not.

Most premium cigars are packaged with thin sheets of cedar between each layer. Those sheets fold and break apart into strips a quarter-inch to a half-inch wide with almost no effort. Those strips are spills, and you've probably been throwing them away.

Factory-made spills are available to buy, but half the appeal of the cedar spill ritual is making them yourself from the sheets that came with the cigars you smoked. If you don't have spare sheets at home, most cigar shops will hand you some for free. They get discarded anyway.

Commercial spills are also sold as conical, hand-planed Spanish cedar strips designed specifically for lighting cigars without imparting odors or tastes from matches or lighters. Brands like Darkfire and Commonwealth sell them in counted boxes, and the shaped geometry helps them burn more consistently than a hand-torn strip.

## How do I actually make cedar spills from a cigar box sheet?

Start with a flat cedar sheet from a factory box. Let it dry completely if it's been sitting in a humid environment; damp cedar won't ignite cleanly.

You have three practical options:

| Method | Tools needed | Strip quality | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hand-snap | None | Rough, rustic | Quick and casual use |
| Knife & ruler | Exacto knife, cutting board | Clean, even | Most collectors |
| Paper cutter | Long-blade paper trimmer | Very consistent | High volume / gifting |

Using a steel ruler and an Exacto knife on a cutting board, mark your width and slice the sheet into even strips. Aim for strips roughly 1/4 to 3/8 inch wide and 6-9 inches long. Shorter strips burn out before you've finished the toast; thinner strips burn faster than you'd like.

A purpose-made commercial spill burns slower and more consistently than a hand-torn strip, which gives you more time to work. If you're new to the technique, buying a box of commercial spills first gives you a reliable baseline before you start experimenting with homemade ones.

## How do I light a cigar with a cedar spill, step by step?

*A question we hear often:* "I tried a cedar spill once and it burned out before the cigar was lit. What went wrong?"

The angle is almost always the culprit. Hold the spill at a slightly downward angle, because the flame travels upward. Hold it straight up and it burns out fast, before you've done anything useful.

Here's the full sequence:

1. **Light the spill** from a match or candle, not directly from a torch flame, which can scorch the tip of the spill unevenly.
2. **Let the flame stabilize** for two or three seconds until it settles into a steady orange triangle rather than a volatile flicker.
3. **Hold the spill at a 30-45 degree downward angle** in your non-dominant hand. The flame should travel up and away from your fingers.
4. **Rotate the cigar's foot through the flame** in slow, even circles. Keep the cigar out of direct contact with the flame. The spill will burn for roughly a minute, which is enough time to form an even ember.
5. **Puff gently** once the outer ring of the foot glows evenly. No need to rush the first draw.
6. **Dispose of the ash carefully.** The ash from a cedar spill is wispy and light and prone to drifting, though it also tends to extinguish itself quickly.

## Are there situations where a cedar spill is the wrong choice?

Yes, several. A strong breeze will kill the spill mid-toast, leaving you with a half-lit cigar and ash on your shirt. This is an indoor technique, or at most a sheltered patio on a calm evening.

Cedar spills also don't pair well with open-foot vitolas, which are designed to be lit quickly with a direct flame. They work best with a traditional closed-foot cigar where slow, patient toasting pulls heat through the tobacco gradually.

One more caveat: if you're smoking a very mild Connecticut or a delicate wrapper, the cedar note can occasionally read as intrusive on the opening profile. Full-bodied Nicaraguan or Honduran blends tend to absorb that complementary cedar inflection much more gracefully.

## Why does Spanish cedar specifically, and not just any wood, matter?

*Readers frequently ask:* "Can I tear a strip of cedar from my closet organizer and use that?"

No. This matters quite a bit. Spanish cedar has only a faint aroma and low moisture content. Aromatic closet cedar (*Juniperus virginiana*) is saturated with volatile resins and camphor-like oils that will contaminate the tobacco almost immediately.

The cedar sheets inside a cigar box smell pleasant to us, but insects hate Spanish cedar, which is another reason it's so widely used. That natural repellent quality is part of why Spanish cedar lines your humidor, separates box layers, and now lights your cigar without chemical interference.

The wood in commercial spills is the same *Cedrela odorata* species used inside your humidor. If you track your humidor's composition in Stick Picks, you already know your cedar source is working on multiple fronts: humidity regulation, pest deterrence, and lighting ritual.

## Cedar spill vs. torch lighter vs. matches: which is actually best?

There's no single correct answer. It depends on context, preference, and the cigar.

| Method | Flavor impact | Lighting time | Wind resistance | Ritual factor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cedar spill | Faint cedar complement | Slow (45-90 sec) | Poor | Very high |
| Soft-flame lighter | Neutral to slight butane | Moderate | Poor | Low |
| Torch lighter | Neutral (high heat risk) | Fast (20-30 sec) | Excellent | Low |
| Wooden match | Slight sulfur (first 2 sec) | Moderate | Poor | Medium |
| Candle | Neutral (paraffin risk) | Slow | Poor | Medium |

The spill wins on ritual, on flavor cleanliness, and on the slow toasting pace that encourages an even light. The torch wins on speed and outdoor performance. Most collectors keep both: a torch for the patio, a tin of cedar spills for a quiet evening inside.

## How do I store cedar spills so they stay usable?

Keep them dry. A spill stored near your humidor can absorb enough moisture to burn poorly or not at all. Store homemade strips and commercial spills in a small tin or resealable bag at room humidity, somewhere in the 45-55% RH range. Most collectors keep a small lidded box of spills on the desk near their smoking chair, well away from the humidor itself.

## FAQs

**Do cedar spills actually change how a cigar tastes?**
Yes, subtly. Spanish cedar imparts a faint, complementary cedar note during the toast, most noticeable on the first few puffs. It doesn't overpower the cigar; many aficionados say it actually enhances the opening profile of a full-bodied stick.

**Can I use regular cedar or aromatic cedar instead of Spanish cedar?**
No. Aromatic cedar (like closet cedar) has a sharp, camphor-like resin that will ruin the flavor of your cigar. Only Spanish cedar (*Cedrela odorata*), the same wood used in humidor linings and cigar box sheets, should be used for spills.

**How long does a cedar spill burn?**
Commercial cedar spills typically burn for 30-60 seconds, which is more than enough time to fully toast and light a cigar. Homemade spills from thin box sheets burn faster, roughly 20-40 seconds, so work quickly and split thicker sheets if you need more time.

**Where do I get cedar sheets to make my own spills?**
The cedar sheets layered between cigars inside factory boxes are perfect. Most cigar retailers will hand you spare sheets for free; they're typically discarded. You can also buy raw Spanish cedar veneer sheets online in bulk and cut them to size.

**Can I use a cedar spill outdoors?**
Not really. Cedar spills produce a wide, low-temperature flame that's easily extinguished or redirected by wind. Reserve them for indoor or sheltered settings, a calm patio on a windless evening at most.
