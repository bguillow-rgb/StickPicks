A proper light is the handshake between you and everything the blender put into a cigar. Rush it, use the wrong tool, or skip the toast, and even a 95-point stick can smoke hot, uneven, and bitter. Get it right, and the flavors unfold the way they were designed to, third by third, from first draw to final inch.

## What's the actual difference between toasting and lighting?

*A question we hear often:* Most people treat these as the same step. They aren't, and conflating them is the single most common reason a premium cigar starts canoeing within the first inch.

Toasting is the dry-heat warm-up phase. You hold the foot of the cigar roughly half an inch above the flame, never touching, and rotate it slowly for about 10-15 seconds. The goal is to darken the outer ring of the foot to a thin gray-black while leaving the center slightly behind. This brings the wrapper and binder up to temperature before any draw is involved, so when you do pull air through the cigar, the wrapper, binder, and filler ignite together.

Lighting is what happens next: you bring the cigar to your lips, take slow controlled puffs (think drawing liquid through a straw, not hard inhales), and keep the flame near the foot without touching it. You should see the entire foot surface begin to glow evenly. Take the cigar out of your mouth, look at the foot, and gently blow on the ember. A fully lit foot glows orange-red all the way to the edges with no brown, unlit patches.

The whole sequence takes 30-60 seconds for most ring gauges. That patience pays for itself across the next hour.

## Which lighter should I actually use?

| Lighter Type | Pros | Cons | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-jet butane torch | Precise, wind-resistant, odorless | Can scorch if held too close | Touch-ups, lanceros, most everyday cigars |
| Double/triple-jet torch | Faster even coverage on large rings | Less precision for touch-ups | Ring gauge 54+ |
| Soft-flame butane | Cooler flame, good for indoor use | Struggles in any breeze | Indoor sessions, torpedo caps |
| Long sulfur-free wooden matches | Classic, no fuel flavor | Burns fast, needs two hands | Ritual lighting, indoor lounges |
| Cedar spills | Completely flavor-neutral, traditional | Requires advance prep | Special occasions, aged cigars |
| Zippo / fluid lighter | Convenient | Petroleum can taint tobacco flavor | Not recommended |
| Candle / stovetop |, | Adds wax or gas flavors | Never |

The torch lighter is the practical daily driver for most collectors. Butane burns clean and evaporates immediately, leaving no residue behind. According to cigar industry guidance, using an odorless flame is non-negotiable if you want to taste what the blender actually intended. Light the same stick two different ways, once with a torch, once with a Zippo, and the petroleum-lit version will often taste noticeably sharper in the first third.

For wooden matches, let the sulfur tip burn completely off before bringing the match near the cigar. Cigar-specific matches run about four inches long, which gives you the time you need without a relay race to strike another one.

## Does ring gauge change how I light?

*This one comes up a lot:* Yes, significantly. A 42-ring lancero needs a focused, single-jet flame and lights in under 30 seconds. A 60-ring gordo needs broader heat coverage, two or three jet flames, and may take 45-60 seconds of patient toasting to get an even glow all the way to the outer edges.

The rule of thumb: the wider the foot, the more heat distribution you need and the more patient you should be. Rushing a large ring gauge almost guarantees canoeing within the first inch because the center lights first and begins burning ahead of the wrapper edges before they've even properly ignited.

For figurados (torpedoes, pyramids, and perfectos), light from the smallest diameter end. Toast the pointed cap area with a gentle circular motion before drawing.

## Why is my cigar canoeing, and how do I fix it?

*Readers frequently ask:* Canoeing, where one side of the cigar burns significantly faster than the other and carves out a hollow curved channel, is the most common burn problem. It happens because airflow inside the cigar isn't balanced. The faster-burning side is getting more oxygen.

Common causes:
- Uneven toasting at the light
- Wind pushing the flame from one direction
- Puffing consistently from only one side of the cut
- A soft spot in the filler (a construction issue, not your fault)
- A cigar stored at inconsistent humidity that dried unevenly

Fixes:
1. Touch up the slow side. Hold the lighter flame to the lagging side of the foot and give it 5-10 seconds of gentle heat without drawing. Let the burn line re-balance.
2. Rotate while smoking. Turning the cigar a quarter-turn every few minutes promotes even airflow and heat distribution. Worth building as a habit from day one.
3. A tiny amount of moisture on your fingertip, applied lightly to the faster-burning wrapper, slows combustion on that side while the slow side catches up.

If the canoe is severe and past the halfway point, you may decide the cigar isn't worth saving. A well-made cigar from a quality blend, stored at the right humidity, canoes far less frequently. If you're noticing the problem consistently with a particular stick from your humidor, check your storage humidity. Over-humidification causes uneven moisture in the filler, which is a leading cause of erratic burns.

## What about tunneling, the cigar burns through the center but not the wrapper?

Tunneling is the mirror image of canoeing. Instead of one side outrunning the other, the filler burns ahead of the wrapper and binder, hollowing out a tube through the center. The telltale sign is a sharp drop in smoke volume without the cigar going fully out.

Tunneling usually happens for one of three reasons: puffing too infrequently (the wrapper and binder cool while the insulated core keeps smoldering), lighting only the center of the foot instead of the full circumference, or a cigar that was stored too moist. According to published guidance from multiple cigar burn specialists, keeping humidor humidity at 65-70% RH dramatically reduces tunneling frequency versus storage above 72%.

The fix: toast the wrapper edge back to the burn line with your lighter, and pick up your puffing cadence. Roughly one draw every 60-90 seconds keeps everything burning in sync.

## When is it okay to relight a cigar?

A cigar that goes out in the first third can almost always be relit with no flavor penalty, especially if it went out within the last 5-10 minutes. Tap or gently blow off the ash to expose fresh tobacco, purge any stale smoke from the body by blowing lightly through the head end, then re-toast the foot and light exactly as you would from scratch.

A cigar that sat out for 30 minutes or more, or one you're picking back up from a previous session, is a harder call. Stale smoke condenses in the tobacco and can make the relight taste harsh or bitter. Many collectors purge by blowing slowly and firmly through the head several times before re-toasting. If the first couple of puffs still taste acrid, let that smoke escape from your mouth rather than holding it.

Stale relights are one of the reasons keeping a cigar journal is worth the habit. Tracking which sticks you finished in one sitting versus which ones suffered through a relight gives you useful data on what to smoke on a leisurely afternoon versus a quick thirty-minute window.

## Does how long a cigar rested in the humidor affect the light?

Absolutely. A cigar that went straight from a retailer's walk-in cooler to your lighter the same afternoon may not have fully equalized to your humidor's humidity. The filler, binder, and wrapper each absorb moisture at slightly different rates, and a cigar that hasn't rested tends to burn unevenly because those layers aren't in equilibrium with each other.

The general recommendation is to let freshly purchased cigars rest in your humidor for at least two to four weeks before smoking. Cigars that have rested properly light more evenly, hold the cherry better between puffs, and produce a cleaner draw. If you're consistently battling burn issues on new acquisitions specifically, rest time is the first variable to examine. ## Quick-reference: the 5-step light

1. Cut first. You can't taste your light technique on a cigar you can't draw. A clean cut opens the head for even airflow throughout the light.
2. Toast the foot. Flame half an inch below, rotate continuously, 10-15 seconds until the outer edge shows thin, even darkening.
3. First draw. Bring the cigar to your lips, keep the flame near the foot, take 2-3 slow puffs while rotating.
4. Check the ember. Remove from your mouth and look at the foot. Blow gently. Every part should glow. Touch up any dark spots.
5. Settle in. Take a relaxed puff every 60-90 seconds. Don't rush. The first third is when most of the blender's introductory flavor notes appear.

Most experienced collectors will tell you the light is the one part of the ritual that genuinely can't be delegated to muscle memory. Every cigar is slightly different, and paying attention during those first sixty seconds is what separates a great smoke from a frustrating one.
