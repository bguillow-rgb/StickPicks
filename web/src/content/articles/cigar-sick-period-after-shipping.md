Every collector has done it at least once: the package hits the doorstep, you rip it open, clip the cap, and light up, only to get a tight draw, acrid smoke, and that sinking feeling you just wasted a good cigar. That's the sick period at work. Understanding what shipping actually does to a premium handmade cigar, and how to counteract it, is one of the most practical things you can do as a collector.

## What is the "cigar sick period" and why does it happen?

*A question we hear often:* collectors new to buying online notice that cigars they've read glowing reviews about taste harsh or flat right after delivery, and wonder if they got a bad batch.

The sick period is the window of time after shipping during which your cigars underperform because of the temperature and humidity changes they endured in transit. It's not a defect in the cigar itself. It's a stress response in the tobacco.

Here's what's actually happening inside the leaf: a premium handmade cigar has three distinct components, filler, binder, and wrapper, each rolled to different densities and each carrying slightly different moisture levels. Resting lets residual ammonia off-gas and gives those components time to behave as one cigar rather than three separate layers reacting against each other. When that equilibrium gets disrupted by transit, the cigar burns unevenly, draws tightly, and releases harsh, vegetal, or acrid compounds instead of the complex flavors it's supposed to.

Worth noting: the sick period isn't exclusive to online retailers. Brick-and-mortar stores receive their cigars the same way you do, through the mail. Your local shop's inventory went through its own sick period before it hit the walk-in humidor.

## What exactly happens to a cigar inside a shipping box?

A cigar bound for your house may sit in a humid retailer's storeroom on Monday, then in a cold air-freight pallet at thirty thousand feet on Tuesday, then in a heated sorting depot on Wednesday, then in the back of a delivery van on a frosty Thursday afternoon, then on your front porch for two hours after the driver leaves.

Cigars in transit face swings in temperature and humidity, plus physical jostling. Good packaging mitigates most of it, but the longer they're in the box, the greater the cumulative risk. The three biggest threats:

1. **Humidity loss.** Even a well-packed box with a Boveda pack inside loses moisture over several days. Extended transit times can quietly pull a cigar below the threshold where it smokes well.
2. **Temperature extremes.** A mail truck in July or a sorting facility in January can both do real damage, especially if the cigar sits there for hours.
3. **Physical stress.** Jostling and pressure from other packages compresses the filler, contributes to draw resistance, and can crack wrappers, particularly on thinner-leafed varieties like Connecticut Shade or Ecuadorian.

Excessive heat can even cause tobacco beetle eggs to hatch inside the cigar. This is why summer shipments deserve extra scrutiny.

## How bad is summer shipping vs. winter shipping?

*This one comes up a lot,* especially from collectors in the South and Southwest watching tracking updates with increasing dread in July and August.

Heat is generally more dangerous than cold. Heat dries cigars out; cold makes wrappers brittle. But the two threats play out differently.

| Condition | Primary Risk | Recovery Time | What to Watch For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Summer heat (80°F+) | Re-fermentation, beetle hatch, wrapper cracking | 3, 4 weeks | Acrid taste, visible cracks, tiny pinholes |
| Winter cold (below 40°F) | Wrapper brittleness, cracking on rapid warm-up | 1, 2 weeks | Surface cracks if opened too soon |
| High humidity in transit | Mold, soggy draw, burn problems | 2, 3 weeks | Soft spots, white bloom |
| Rapid humidity loss | Dry, tight draw; flat flavor | 1, 3 weeks | Wrapper separation, papery feel |
| Long transit (7+ days) | Compound stress from multiple factors | 3, 4 weeks minimum | All of the above in combination |

Extreme swings in heat and humidity cause the tobacco to swell, which can crack wrappers outright.

For cold-weather arrivals: when a shipment has been through very hot or very cold conditions, leave it sealed indoors for a few hours before opening. Then inspect the cigars and let them sit in quarantine for a few days in a gallon zipper bag with a small humidifier before they go anywhere near your main humidor.

## What does a stressed cigar actually taste like?

*Readers frequently ask* how to tell if their cigars are just in the sick period versus actually damaged, because the difference matters.

A cigar in its sick period typically shows one or more of these:

- **Tight or plugged draw:** filler is compressed and moisture hasn't redistributed yet
- **Acrid, ammonia-like sharpness:** residual fermentation compounds that haven't off-gassed
- **Flat or muted flavor:** the blend's complexity is literally suppressed by moisture imbalance
- **Uneven burn or tunneling:** the three layers are at different moisture levels, so they combust at different rates

Cigars that have been through fluctuating RH and temperature just need time to settle. Not resting them often produces a bad draw, and the wrong humidity level affects flavor in ways that are hard to separate from actual blend character. The improvement, when it comes, tends to show up first in draw and burn, then flavor.

The good news: with enough rest in a stable environment, most of this resolves. The question is how much rest.

## How long should I actually wait before smoking?

Under normal domestic shipping conditions, 10, 14 days in your humidor is usually enough to get a cigar back to where it should be. The answer depends on how much stress it went through, though. For more demanding situations:

- **Local or regional shop purchase (well-maintained humidor):** same day to 48 hours is fine
- **Standard domestic online order, 3, 5 days transit:** 10, 14 days minimum
- **Summer shipment or long transit (5, 10 days):** 3, 4 weeks
- **International order or extended customs hold:** rest the cigar for at least twice the time it spent in transit. A cigar that took ten days to arrive, including a customs hold, gets twenty days minimum on top of basic acclimatization.

One distinction worth keeping in mind: aging starts at a year or more; anything less than that is resting. Resting is recovery. Aging is transformation. You need to do the first before the second can work properly.

## What's the right way to receive and quarantine a shipment?

*A question we hear often* from collectors who've had a beetle outbreak or mold incident trace back to a new arrival.

Here is a reliable arrival protocol:

**Step 1: Let the box equalize.** If the package arrived in extreme heat or cold, leave it sealed indoors for 1, 2 hours before opening. This prevents condensation from forming on cold tobacco as it hits warm air.

**Step 2: Inspect before anything goes into your main humidor.** Visible damage to the wrapper, cracks, soft spots, discoloration, means something is wrong. Check the foot of each cigar for the sharp ammonia smell that signals the sick period is active. If a cigar looks or smells bad, give it more time before making any decisions about it.

**Step 3: Quarantine.** Leave new arrivals in a sealed bag with a Boveda pouch for a week or two, then move them to your main humidor to continue resting. This gives you time to catch mold or pest problems before they touch your established collection.

**Step 4: Date your arrivals.** This is where a tool like [Stick Picks](https://stickpicks.app) earns its keep: log each incoming box with an arrival date and a note on shipping conditions, so you know exactly when the rest period is up and which sticks are ready versus which need more time.

**Step 5: Check humidity, not your phone's tracking page.** Somewhere between 65% and 69% RH works for most modern blends. The exact number matters less than keeping it stable. Use a calibrated hygrometer, not the cheap one that came packaged with your humidor.

## Can shipping damage be permanent?

In most cases, no. Tobacco is a resilient agricultural product, and cigars travel long paths before they even reach the United States, let alone your door. They're more durable than their price tags sometimes suggest.

That said, some failure modes don't recover with rest alone:

- If you see tiny holes from tobacco beetles, throw the cigar away. It's not worth risking your entire collection.
- Wrapper splits that go through to the binder rarely heal cleanly and will cause draw and burn problems throughout the smoke.
- Mold is a disqualifier, not a recoverable condition the way the sick period is. Excessive humidity in transit can make cigars soggy and susceptible to it, and once mold takes hold it spreads.

The practical takeaway: if a cigar still smells acrid or draws poorly after four full weeks in a stable humidor at 65, 70% RH, it may be damaged beyond recovery. Smoke it anyway and see. The worst outcome is a subpar experience on a single stick, which is still useful information.

## Does the "right off the truck" temptation ever work out?

Sometimes. Not reliably, and not in your favor on average. Smoking new arrivals right off the truck almost always ends in regret, particularly if you're newer to the hobby.

The collector who keeps 3, 6 months of cigars ahead of their smoking pace never has to smoke ROTT under pressure. The newest ones rest while the oldest ones get smoked, so the sick period never affects what you're actually lighting up. That's the real long-term fix: build enough inventory depth that patience isn't a sacrifice.

Tracking what's rested and what isn't, across dozens of cigars from multiple orders, is exactly the kind of task where a humidor app pays off, so you're not relying on memory or sticky notes stuck to a cedar tray.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the cigar sick period?**
The sick period is the window after shipping when a cigar underperforms, tasting harsh, acrid, or flat, because transit stress has disrupted its internal moisture balance and triggered minor re-fermentation. It typically lasts 1, 4 weeks in a properly maintained humidor.

**How long should cigars rest after shipping?**
Most cigars need a minimum of 10, 14 days in a stable humidor after delivery. Summer shipments, international orders, or packages exposed to extreme temperatures may need 3, 4 weeks. As a general rule, rest at least twice as long as the time the cigar spent in transit under difficult conditions.

**Can cigars be permanently damaged by shipping heat?**
In most cases, no. Sustained exposure above 80°F (27°C) can cause wrapper cracking, re-fermentation, and tobacco beetle eggs to hatch, and extreme or prolonged heat damage may not fully recover with rest alone. But outright permanent damage is less common than collectors fear.

**Is it safe to smoke a cigar right out of the shipping box?**
You can, but you probably shouldn't. Smoking right off the truck (ROTT) almost always delivers a harsher, less nuanced experience because the cigar's three layers haven't equilibrated after transit stress, leading to uneven burns, tight draws, and muted or acrid flavor.

**Should I quarantine new cigars before adding them to my main humidor?**
Yes, for any box arriving from an unfamiliar source or after a long or difficult transit. A 5, 7 day quarantine in a sealed zip-lock bag or small secondary humidor with a Boveda pack lets you catch mold or pest issues before they spread to your established collection.
