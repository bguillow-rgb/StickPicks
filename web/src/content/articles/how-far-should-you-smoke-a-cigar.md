There's a moment every cigar smoker eventually faces: the stick is burning well, the flavors are building, and you're wondering exactly when to put it down. It's a deceptively simple question, and getting it wrong in either direction means wasting good tobacco or suffering through a hot, harsh final third that ruins an otherwise great smoke.

## So, how far down should I actually smoke a cigar?

The practical rule most experienced smokers follow is to stop when 1 to 2 inches of cigar remain, measured from the lit foot toward the head. At that point, heat has nowhere left to dissipate, and the remaining tobacco has absorbed a session's worth of tars, condensed oils, and combustion byproducts. The result is a noticeably sharper, more acrid smoke that most people find unpleasant.

A useful secondary cue is the cigar band. On most premium cigars, the band sits roughly 2 inches from the head, which puts it in the ballpark of a sensible stopping point. That said, bands are applied at different positions across different brands and vitolas, so treat the band as a rough marker, not a hard rule. Your two most reliable signals are temperature and flavor: when the cigar becomes uncomfortably warm to hold, or when the taste shifts from complex and pleasant to sharp and bitter, the cigar has told you it's done.

## Why does a cigar get harsh and hot toward the end?

*A question we hear often:* collectors new to premium cigars are sometimes confused when a cigar that smoked beautifully through the first two-thirds suddenly turns acrid in the final stretch.

The physics are simple, even if the chemistry is complex. Throughout a smoke, combustion produces condensed tars, ammonia compounds, nicotine, and various essential oils that migrate down through the unsmoked tobacco, collecting ahead of the burn line. By the time you reach the final inch or two, that tobacco has been acting as a filter for the entire preceding smoke. It's saturated. The remaining tobacco mass also can't absorb and disperse heat as effectively, so burn temperature climbs. Studies of combustion chemistry in cured tobacco leaf consistently show that polycyclic compounds and tar concentrations spike sharply in the final third of a session, which tracks with what every experienced smoker notices subjectively: the last inch or two almost always smokes hotter and harsher than the rest.

This is also why pacing matters throughout the entire smoke, not just at the end. Smoking too quickly accelerates heat buildup in the still-unlit tobacco ahead of the burn, pre-loading that final stretch with even more concentrated compounds. A measured 60-to-90-second interval between puffs gives the cigar time to cool slightly and the smoke more time to develop complexity. ## Does the size or strength of a cigar change where I should stop?

*This one comes up a lot:* whether a Robusto and a Churchill require the same stopping point.

Yes and no. The 1-to-2-inch guideline holds across most formats, but a few variables shift things meaningfully:

| Cigar type / characteristic | Practical stopping point | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mild, Connecticut-shade wrapper | Closer to 1 inch remaining | Lower tar load; smoother heat buildup allows slightly more latitude |
| Full-bodied, ligero-heavy blend | Closer to 2 inches remaining | Higher nicotine and oil concentration accelerates harshness in the final stretch |
| Large ring gauge (54+) | 2 inches or more | More filler = more accumulated condensate migrating forward |
| Lancero / smaller ring gauge (38-42) | Around 1.5 inches | Narrower format heats faster; less mass to buffer temperature |
| Infused or flavored cigar | 2+ inches | Flavoring compounds can intensify unpleasantly as heat rises |
| Maduro wrapper | 1.5-2 inches | Naturally sweeter and more oily; still accumulates harshness, just later |

Pay attention to the cigar in your hand rather than a fixed measurement. A great full-bodied [Nicaraguan blend](https://stickpicks.app/articles/nicaraguan-vs-dominican-cigars) may demand more margin than a mellow Dominican morning smoke.

## What exactly is a "nub," and do some smokers actually prize it?

*Readers frequently ask:* whether the word "nub" is an insult (as in, the leftover stub nobody wants) or something collectors genuinely pursue.

Both, depending on context. In the most neutral sense, a nub is the short remnant left after most of the cigar has been consumed, typically that final 1 to 2 inches. The term carries no negative connotation among serious collectors. In fact, the final third of many premium cigars is often the most flavor-intensive, as the complexity of the blend reaches its peak before the heat takes over.

Some collectors embrace the nub so enthusiastically that they use a dedicated nub holder, a small metal or wooden tube that clips or slides onto the remaining inch of cigar so you can hold it without burning your fingers. This is especially common with high-priced sticks where leaving a full inch on the ashtray feels like abandoning a small fortune. A single high-end limited-edition cigar can retail anywhere from $30 to well over $100, which puts the economics of a good nub holder in an entirely different light.

There's also an entire brand named after the concept: Nub Cigars (by Oliva), which popularized the idea of a short, wide-ring-gauge format designed to deliver the concentrated flavors of a nub right from the first puff. Available in 4x60 and 4x64 formats, they essentially begin where most cigars end.

## Should I stub a cigar out like a cigarette when I'm done?

No, and this matters more than most people realize. A cigar should be set down and allowed to go out on its own. Stubbing it out crushes the remaining tobacco and releases a sharp burst of acrid smoke and tar into your immediate environment (and into the wrapper itself, should you plan to relight it). Most premium handmade cigars will extinguish naturally within two to three minutes of being set down.

According to general industry guidance from IPCPR-affiliated retailers, cigars should never be stubbed out, twisted, or ground against an ashtray. Doing so is considered poor form at a lounge and genuinely damaging to the aroma lingering in the room. Set it down, let it die, then enjoy the lingering room note as part of the experience. ## How do I know when a cigar is officially "done"?

*A question we hear often:* especially from collectors who feel guilty setting down a good cigar before the very end.

Three reliable signals, in order of reliability:

1. Heat. If the cigar is too warm to comfortably hold within two inches of the band, it's done. No amount of great flavor is worth burning your fingers or lips.
2. Flavor shift. When the pleasant complexity you've been enjoying gives way to a sharp, ammonia-edged, or bitter character, the cigar is communicating that you've reached the end of the enjoyable window.
3. Draw resistance. In the final inch, draw resistance often increases as the dense, saturated tobacco offers more restriction. A noticeably tighter or hotter draw is another signal to put it down.

Tracking where your favorite cigars reach this point is genuinely useful data for a collector. ## Frequently asked questions

**What is a cigar nub?**
A cigar nub is the short remnant left after most of a cigar has been smoked, typically the final 1 to 2 inches. Some collectors prize a good nub for its concentrated flavors; others set the cigar down before that point to avoid harshness.

**Should I smoke a cigar all the way to the end?**
No. Smoking past the 1-to-2-inch mark causes rapid heat buildup and concentrates tar, ammonia, and harsh compounds in the remaining tobacco. The experience degrades noticeably, and the cigar becomes uncomfortable to hold.

**Does the band mark where you should stop smoking?**
The band is a useful rough reference. Many smokers stop just at or slightly below it, but bands are placed at different positions on different cigars, so use heat and flavor as your primary signals, not the band's position alone.

**Why does a cigar get harsh toward the end?**
As you smoke, tars, oils, and combustion byproducts accumulate in the remaining tobacco and binder. The shorter the cigar gets, the hotter it burns and the more concentrated those compounds become, creating a sharp, bitter, and sometimes harsh smoke.

**What is a cigar nub holder and do I need one?**
A nub holder (or cigar holder) is a small accessory, often a metal or wooden tube, that lets you grip the very last inch of a cigar without burning your fingers. They're popular with collectors who enjoy smoking high-quality sticks all the way down. Not essential, but a fun addition for the enthusiast who hates wasting good leaf.
