Factory seconds and overruns are one of the hobby's most genuinely useful secrets. The kind of thing experienced collectors mention almost in a whisper at the cigar lounge, then quietly stock their humidors with. If you've ever spotted a bundle of unbranded sticks priced at $2, $3 apiece from a maker you recognize and wondered whether you were getting a deal or getting burned, this guide is for you.

## So what exactly *are* cigar factory seconds?

Cigar seconds, also called *cigar 2nds* or *factory seconds*, are cigars that didn't pass the quality-control test at the factory, or cigars that were simply overproduced. That doesn't mean they're bad cigars.

The most common reason a cigar gets classified as a second is its appearance. The wrapper is the most prominent leaf and it sells the cigar. Many makers, like Rocky Patel, Manuel Quesada, and Nick Perdomo, inspect each cigar for color discrepancies. Too light or too dark? Set it aside. Sunspots or noticeable veins? Same. Generally, none of that affects how the cigar smokes, only how it looks.

Some cigars get labeled seconds after rolling. Because premium cigars are handmade, human error is part of the process. A cigar may come out too thick, too thin, too short, with a tighter draw than spec, or occasionally blended with the wrong leaves. Rather than scrapping them, the factory sells them at a discount.

## What's the difference between a second and an overrun?

*A question we hear often:* collectors conflate these two categories, but they represent meaningfully different things.

Overruns are their own category. When producing millions of handmade cigars each year, factories sometimes produce more than the market ordered. The surplus gets sold off, sometimes called "cigar liquidation." Overruns are not seconds or mistakes. They are simply overproduced cigars. Even the finest lines from a given maker can end up in an overrun situation.

When you buy an overrun, you are often getting a 100% first-quality premium cigar with nothing missing except the paper band and the wooden box. That distinction matters. If an overrun is purely the result of surplus production, you're essentially buying the same cigar you'd find in a $300 box for a fraction of the cost, with zero cosmetic compromise.

| Feature | First-Run Cigar | Factory Second | Factory Overrun |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tobacco blend | Premium, fully aged | Same blend & age | Identical, first quality |
| Construction | Strictly inspected | Usually solid; rare draw issues | Fully inspected, passed |
| Appearance | Flawless wrapper | Minor spots, veins, or color variance | Perfect, just no band |
| Packaging | Branded box + band | Paper bundle, no band | Paper bundle, no band |
| Typical price | $9, $18+ per stick | $2, $4 per stick | $2, $5 per stick |

## Are cigar seconds actually worth buying?

*This one comes up a lot:* the honest answer is that it depends on what you want from the purchase.

Factory seconds and overruns can be very worth buying, but only if you care more about value than aesthetic perfection, and only if you know what you're purchasing. For collectors who want a daily driver, something to smoke on the porch on a Tuesday afternoon without watching a $15 stick go up in smoke, a well-sourced second from a maker like Oliva or AJ Fernandez can be genuinely excellent.

The catch is consistency. Some seconds are a real crapshoot, while others are reliably the same from bundle to bundle. Rocky Patel and Oliva seconds tend to be consistent across the board. AJF and PDR are more of a literal gamble, where you can only choose the vitola. Know your source and read what the cigar community says before committing to a bundle.

Some enthusiasts dismiss anything labeled a second, mistake, or overrun on principle. That's often a mistake. Factory seconds are a legitimate way to pick up a first-class cigar at a very different price point.

## Why do they come without bands, and does that matter?

Factory seconds and overruns are typically bundled and sold without the band from the line they came from. The absence of a band is partly practical and partly legal. Manufacturers don't want unbranded goods mistaken for, or passed off as, their premium boxed product.

For collectors who catalog their humidors, this creates a minor problem: you can't scan a band that doesn't exist. Some retailers do disclose the source line, which makes journaling easier. If you use Stick Picks to track your collection, the entry is still worth filling out. Note the retailer, the bundle origin, the vitola, and your tasting impressions. That record becomes useful when you're deciding whether to reorder six months later.

## Do seconds and overruns smoke the same as their banded siblings?

*Readers frequently ask:* this is where real-world experience diverges from simple theory.

Most forum veterans will tell you that seconds need time. They can taste a little immature right out of the bundle, but after a few weeks in the humidor they come around and match up well with their banded counterparts. The same tobacco is there. It just needs to acclimate, especially if the bundle shipped without the cedar box and individual cellophane that normally buffer a cigar in transit.

Most smoke well and have no problems with caps or wrappers. The occasional tight stick shows up and needs a few passes with a draw tool, but that's the exception. Most are probably seconds only because of wrapper appearance, which means the actual smoking experience is typically indistinguishable from the full-priced version once the cigar has rested.

Plan for a minimum of two to three weeks in a properly seasoned humidor before smoking your first stick from a new bundle. Some collectors give them a full month, especially if they arrived noticeably dry. ## When should you *avoid* cigar seconds?

Seconds are not always the right call. A few scenarios where you're better off buying retail:

- **Special occasions.** If you're smoking a cigar to mark a milestone, you probably want the full presentation, pristine wrapper, the band, the box. The ritual matters.
- **Aging long-term.** The provenance gap is real. When you pull a cigar from a 5-year aging experiment, knowing its exact origin and production date has value. Unidentified seconds can't provide that. Our [cigar aging guide](https://stickpicks.app/articles/how-long-to-age-cigars) covers what to look for in cigars worth laying down.
- **Gifting.** Handing someone an unbanded, paper-bundled cigar reads as careless unless you explain the backstory. For gifting advice, see our [how to gift cigars guide](https://stickpicks.app/articles/how-to-gift-cigars).
- **Unknown sources.** If a retailer won't tell you even the country of origin of the tobacco, that's a red flag. Transparency varies a lot by vendor.

## Where do collectors buy factory seconds and overruns?

The main legitimate channels are established online cigar retailers with standing relationships with manufacturers. Some retailers maintain dedicated sections where you'll find factory 2nds and overruns from well-known makers. The cigars get set aside and sold for a number of reasons that often have little effect on flavor or performance.

You can find premium tobacco from makers like Oliva, Rocky Patel, or AJ Fernandez for a fraction of what their first-run, banded cigars cost. Pricing in the $2, $4 per stick range is common for authenticated seconds from major Honduran and Nicaraguan producers. Seconds usually come in bundles of 10, 15, 20, or more. Buying a 20-count bundle is a sensible test. Smoke one on arrival as a baseline, rest the rest, and evaluate improvement over four to six weeks.

## FAQs

**Are cigar factory seconds the same tobacco as the regular line?**
In most cases, yes. Seconds are rejected for visual reasons, wrapper color, minor veins, or slight size variance, not because the tobacco blend or fermentation is different. The filler and binder inside are typically identical to the full-priced version.

**What is the difference between a cigar second and a cigar overrun?**
A second failed a cosmetic quality-control check after rolling. An overrun is a fully first-quality cigar that was simply overproduced, no defect at all. Overruns are often considered the better buy because the only thing missing is the band and the box.

**Do cigar seconds need extra time in the humidor before smoking?**
Yes. Bundle-packed seconds and overruns often arrive drier or more humid than boxed cigars due to less protective packaging. Plan for at least two to three weeks of resting time in a properly seasoned humidor before your first smoke.

**Can I tell which brand a cigar second comes from?**
Sometimes, but not always. Some retailers are transparent (Alec Bradley, Oliva, and Rocky Patel seconds are often identified by source). Others are sold under generic regional labels like "Nicaraguan 90+ Seconds" where the specific line is deliberately not disclosed.

**Should I age cigar factory seconds or smoke them right away?**
Give them at least a few weeks in the humidor first. Many collectors report that seconds tasted harsh or underdeveloped on arrival but improved noticeably after two to four weeks of rest. For longer aging, treat them exactly as you would any premium cigar, same RH, same temperature, same rotation.
