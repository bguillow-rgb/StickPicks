Yes, you can relight a cigar. Whether you *should*, and how well it goes, depends almost entirely on how you put it out, how long it sat, and how carefully you rekindle it. Done right, a same-day relight is a perfectly acceptable experience. Done wrong, it tastes like an ashtray. Here's exactly how to do it right.

## Why does a cigar go out in the first place?

*A question we hear often:* Before worrying about relighting, it helps to understand why the cigar went out.

The most common reason is pacing. Get absorbed in a conversation or a long game and a cigar will extinguish on its own within a few minutes. You stop puffing, the ember dies, that's it. A less obvious culprit is humidity: if your cigar was over-humidified to begin with, keeping it lit consistently is genuinely harder throughout the smoke.

Construction is a third category. If the cigar is plugged or burning unevenly, it may have been assembled with an inconsistent pack, in which case you're probably better off with a fresh stick. For the first two causes, though, a relight is a reasonable fix.

## What actually happens to a cigar when it goes out?

*This one comes up a lot:* Many collectors assume a resting cigar is essentially "paused." It isn't.

When a cigar goes out, the tobacco keeps changing. As it cools, tars, oils, and other residues from combustion settle and concentrate in the unsmoked tobacco ahead of the char line. The longer it sits, the worse this gets. A half-smoked cigar will almost never taste better the second time around. It won't develop overnight the way a well-rested stew might. Once lit, its peak is behind it.

That doesn't mean a relight is futile. The first few draws might carry a faint charred note, but a clean cut of the burnt end and proper technique can recover most of the original profile. Timing is everything: same-day relights are generally fine, next-day ones are a compromise at best.

## How do I properly put out a cigar I want to save?

The way you extinguish a cigar determines how well it comes back. Get this step wrong and no amount of careful relighting recovers the smoke.

Stamping a cigar out can crack the wrapper, dislodge filler, and create off-flavors that don't burn clean. Instead, tap off any existing ash, rest the lit cigar in an ashtray divot, and let the ember die on its own. It takes a few minutes. Don't rush it.

Never treat a cigar like a cigarette when putting it out. Improperly snuffing one creates an unpleasant taste and an uneven burn when you try to relight.

Once the ember is cold (give it at least five to ten minutes), you're ready to think about storage.

## Where should I store a half-smoked cigar?

*Readers frequently ask:* Can I just tuck it back in my humidor to keep it fresh?

No. Never store a half-smoked cigar in your humidor. The stale smoke residue will transfer its odor to your fresh collection, and in a well-sealed humidor that smell can take a very long time to dissipate. This is one of those rules experienced collectors learn the hard way, usually once.

The practical alternative is simpler than most people expect. Let the cigar cool fully, clip the burnt end, then place it in a small plastic bag or its original cellophane wrapper at room temperature. A dedicated travel case or a cigar tube also works. The goal is to contain the odor and limit further oxidation, not to replicate full humidor conditions for a cigar you're planning to finish within hours.

Smoke it within a day or two. The longer you wait, the more the flavor deteriorates. For best results, relight within 24 hours.

## How do I relight a cigar correctly?

This is where most people shortcut the process and then blame the cigar for tasting harsh.

**Step 1: Trim the burnt end.** Clip the charred foot before applying any flame. Trim about a quarter-inch off with a clean cutter. This removes residual bitterness and helps the cigar relight evenly.

**Step 2: Purge.** Before lighting, blow gently through the head of the cigar. This pushes stale, oxidized smoke out of the filler and cuts down significantly on harsh first draws. A cedar spill or butane torch works well here because both produce a neutral flame.

**Step 3: Toast slowly.** Jabbing a flame at the foot and drawing hard scorches the tobacco and introduces harsh compounds. A slow rotational toast, heat hovering near the foot without direct contact, turning the cigar evenly, produces a far cleaner restart.

**Step 4: Take it easy on the first draws.** Don't rush. Expect the first two or three draws to carry some bitterness. That's the old smoke and oxidized residue burning off. Most smokers purge through it and the cigar comes back to something close to where it was.

## Does the cigar type or size affect how well it relights?

| Factor | Better for relighting | Worse for relighting |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Smaller vitolas (Corona, Robusto) | Larger formats (Churchill, Double Corona) |
| Time since going out | Under 2 hours | Over 12 hours |
| Storage after going out | Sealed bag, cool temp, upright | Open air, warm room, on its side |
| Wrapper style | Mild/natural wrappers | Very oily, complex Maduros* |
| Filler construction | Even, well-packed | Loose or plugged from the start |

*\*Community opinion is split on Maduros. Some collectors argue Maduro-wrapped cigars handle an overnight rest better because of the density and natural sweetness of the leaf slowing oxidation. That said, this is community belief more than documented fact, and some sources suggest milder cigars are actually more forgiving on a relight because there's less complexity to lose.*

If you only have thirty minutes, skip the Churchill and reach for a Corona or Robusto. Matching vitola to your available window in the first place is the simplest way to avoid needing to save a cigar at all.

## Is it ever just not worth relighting?

*A question we hear often:* When should you just set a cigar aside permanently?

If you extinguish it properly, trim the burn line, purge before relighting, and take your time, you can salvage a satisfying experience. But go in expecting perfection and you'll probably be disappointed.

Practically: if a cigar has been sitting for more than 24 hours without proper storage, or if it already had construction problems before it went out, move on. A fresh stick from a well-maintained humidor beats a labored relight every time. According to survey data from Cigar Aficionado, over 92 percent of cigar enthusiasts smoke an average of one cigar a day or fewer, which means most collectors have another great cigar resting and ready. Use it.

## FAQs

**Can you put a half-smoked cigar back in your humidor?**
No. A smoked cigar carries stale smoke residue and char odor that will transfer to every other cigar in a well-sealed humidor. Store it separately, in its original cellophane, a small zip bag, or a dedicated travel case, away from your fresh collection.

**How long can a cigar sit before it's too late to relight?**
Most aficionados agree same-day relights (within 1-3 hours) preserve the most flavor. Next-day relights are possible but noticeably harsher. Beyond 24-48 hours, the oils oxidize and the experience deteriorates significantly enough that most collectors simply move on to a fresh stick.

**Does relighting a cigar ruin the flavor?**
It changes it, not necessarily ruins it. The first few draws after a relight carry residual bitterness as old smoke and oxidized tars burn off. A proper purge, a fresh cut of the burnt end, and a slow rotational toast can recover most of the core flavor profile.

**What's the best way to extinguish a cigar you plan to relight?**
Set the cigar on an ashtray divot and stop puffing. It will self-extinguish within a few minutes. Never stub it out like a cigarette; that crushes the filler, cracks the wrapper, and makes a clean relight far harder.

**Should I trim the burnt end before relighting a cigar?**
Yes. Trimming about a quarter-inch off the charred foot removes concentrated tars and oxidized residue. Use a straight cutter or clean cigar scissors. This single step makes more difference to the relight flavor than almost anything else.
