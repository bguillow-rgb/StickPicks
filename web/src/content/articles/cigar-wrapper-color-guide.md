Cigar wrapper color is one of the first things you notice when you open a box, and one of the most misread signals in the hobby. A lot of collectors assume a dark wrapper means a strong cigar, or that a green one is some kind of novelty accident. Neither is true. Wrapper color is a precise, meaningful indicator of how a leaf was grown, cured, and fermented. Once you understand the system behind it, your eye for a humidor tray gets sharper fast.

---

## What is the Colorado Scale, and why should I care about it?

The Colorado Scale is the industry's standard color classification for cigar wrapper leaves. It runs from lightest to darkest across seven named categories, and virtually every serious discussion of wrapper shade maps back to it.

Cigar wrapper colors follow the Colorado Scale, seven shades running from light to dark: Candela (green), Claro (tan), Colorado Claro (warm brown), Colorado (red-brown), Colorado Maduro (dark brown), Maduro (very dark), and Oscuro (near black).

Knowing these seven categories does two practical things for a collector. First, it gives you a shared vocabulary when buying, trading, or cataloguing cigars, especially useful if you're logging your stash in an app like Stick Picks, where wrapper shade is part of the cigar's identity record. Second, it ties color directly back to leaf production method, which is a shortcut to predicting flavor character before you ever light up.

One important caveat: cigar manufacturers have identified roughly a hundred or so different wrapper colors and names over the years, but they basically boil down to eight basic categories, and there are some variations in each. What one company calls a Colorado Claro might be classified as a plain Claro by another, but typically not enough to result in a discernible difference in strength and flavor. Use the scale as a guide, not a rulebook.

## What does each wrapper color actually look like, and taste like?

*This one comes up a lot:* collectors know the name but can't picture the shade on the leaf. Here's the full spectrum, light to dark.

### Candela (Double Claro): green

Double Claro, also called Candela, ranges from very light green to light greenish-brown. The leaves are picked before they're fully developed and quickly heat-cured to lock in that distinctive color. The name "Candela" comes from the traditional drying method of candlelight. Flavor is notably grassy, fresh, and very mild: think cut hay and light citrus. This wrapper was very popular in the U.S. in the 1950s, so it's sometimes also called American Market Selection, or AMS.

### Claro: light tan to golden

Known alternatively as Connecticut Shade, these tobacco leaves are grown under cheesecloth tents and harvested early. Air-dried, Claro leaves come out a natural tan or golden color, and cigars wrapped in them have a mild, aromatic cedar flavor. This is the shade most commonly associated with beginner-friendly Connecticut Shade cigars: creamy, smooth, and low in spice.

### Colorado Claro: warm reddish-brown ("Natural")

The process here is straightforward: cultivate the leaves in direct sunlight and let them mature. These leaves are also called Natural, and the resulting wrappers are neither too dark nor too light. They're typically aged longer and tend to deliver a medium-bodied smoke, with a balance of sweetness, spice, and sometimes earthy or woody notes. When a retailer label just says "Natural," this is usually what it means.

### Colorado (Rosado): medium red-brown

Ranging from medium-brown to dark reddish-brown, Colorado leaves are often left to mature fully in the shade longer than their lighter-colored counterparts. That extra time produces a strong aroma and a rich flavor, making these leaves a popular wrapper choice. Expect notes of roasted nuts, rich earth, cocoa, and a natural sweetness with moderate spice. Many Cuban cigars fall in this range: most Cuban wrappers sit in the Colorado Claro to Colorado Maduro color range, appearing as medium to reddish-brown with an oily sheen.

### Colorado Maduro: dark brown with red tint

Sitting between Colorado and full Maduro, this shade is often the sweet spot for collectors who want complexity and richness without the extreme sweetness of a true Maduro. Color is dark brown with a reddish tint, with flavor notes including cocoa, spice, earth, and coffee.

### Maduro: very dark brown

The Maduro wrapper earns its color through extended fermentation, sometimes lasting months longer than a natural wrapper. The process converts oils and starches into sugars, which is why Maduros taste sweet despite their dark, almost intimidating appearance. See our guide to Maduro cigars for a deep dive into the fermentation process behind this shade.

### Oscuro (Double Maduro): near black

Oscuro, or Double Maduro, is the darkest shade on the Colorado Scale. These wrappers undergo the most extended fermentation of any category, producing an intensely rich, earthy, and sometimes bitter-edged profile. They are rare, prized by experienced collectors, and not for the faint-hearted.

## Does wrapper color tell me how strong the cigar will be?

*A question we hear often:* and the answer is a firm "not reliably." Wrapper color reflects leaf processing, not nicotine load. The wrapper and its darkness are not going to necessarily tell you the strength of a cigar. Hypothetically, you could take a more "natural" or Claro shade Connecticut wrapper and blend it with a binder and filler to achieve a full-strength product.

This is one of the most common misconceptions among newer collectors. A pitch-black Oscuro wrapper on a cigar with a light Seco-dominant filler can be relatively mild. A golden Claro-wrapped stick can pack serious nicotine if the maker piled in Ligero. Wrapper contributes an estimated 30-40% of a cigar's overall flavor character, according to blenders' commonly cited guidelines, but strength is primarily determined by the filler leaves and their primings.

The practical takeaway for your humidor: don't sort by color as a proxy for strength. Sort by what you actually know about the blend. Use your tasting notes, a cigar journal or the journaling feature in Stick Picks, to track how wrapper shade plays into your personal experience across different blends and vitolas. Over time, you'll develop genuine intuitions that go far beyond color alone.

## How does wrapper color relate to how the leaf was grown?

*Readers frequently ask:* why does the same farm produce such different-looking leaves? The answer is mostly sun exposure and fermentation duration.

The fermentation period for shade-grown wrappers, or those grown under natural cloud cover such as in Ecuador, is definitively shorter because these plants have less essential oils and thinner leaves. Sun-grown tobacco, especially with leaves taken from the top half of the plant, or Broadleaf, has a longer fermentation period due to stronger leaves and a higher concentration of essential oils.

The result is a direct link between growing method and color: shade-growing produces leaves toward the Claro end of the spectrum. Sun-growing with extended fermentation pushes toward Colorado and darker. This is why Ecuadorian Connecticut, grown under overcast highland skies, produces the characteristic light-tan, creamy Claro shade, while Dominican and Nicaraguan sun-grown leaves often come off the plant looking considerably darker.

| Shade | Colorado Scale Name | Growing Method | Fermentation | Flavor Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Green | Candela | Harvested early, heat-cured fast | Minimal | Grassy, fresh, very mild |
| Tan/Golden | Claro | Shade-grown (cheesecloth) | Short | Creamy, cedar, smooth |
| Warm Brown | Colorado Claro (Natural) | Sun-grown, early harvest | Medium | Balanced, earthy, light spice |
| Red-Brown | Colorado (Rosado) | Sun/shade, long maturation | Medium-long | Roasted nuts, cocoa, rich earth |
| Dark Brown + Red | Colorado Maduro | Sun-grown, extended | Long | Cocoa, coffee, complex spice |
| Very Dark Brown | Maduro | Any origin, extreme fermentation | Very long | Sweet, rich, full-bodied |
| Near Black | Oscuro | Sun-grown + maximum ferment | Maximum | Intense earth, leather, bitter-edged |

## What does "Natural" on a cigar box label actually mean?

This is where terminology gets genuinely confusing, because "Natural" is used loosely across the industry. Cigar Aficionado's glossary defines natural narrowly as a medium-brown wrapper, the same as Colorado Claro, while others apply the name to anything from Claro to Maduro to Oscuro. In retail practice, "Natural" and Maduro are the two main classes in the market, and Natural covers everything from Claro to Colorado Rosado.

So when a box says "Natural," it means: not Maduro, not Candela, somewhere in the middle of the Colorado Scale. If the precise shade matters to you (it does for aging predictions and pairing decisions), look for the specific varietal name: Connecticut Shade, Ecuadorian Sun Grown, Habano, Corojo. Those names are more informative than the catch-all "Natural."

## Which wrapper color should I focus on when building my humidor?

*This one comes up a lot:* especially from collectors moving beyond their first box. The honest answer is to build across the spectrum deliberately rather than defaulting to one shade.

A well-stocked humidor has at least one representative of each broad category: a Claro (Connecticut Shade robusto for mornings or lighter moments), a Colorado or Colorado Claro for everyday smoking, a Colorado Maduro for evenings and food pairings, and a few Maduros for celebratory smokes or cold-weather sessions. Oscuro and Candela are worth trying once; they're conversation pieces as much as smoking experiences.

On the storage side, a standard humidor at roughly 65-70% relative humidity will keep cigars of any shade at their best. Wrapper color doesn't require different storage conditions, but thinner, lighter Claro leaves can crack faster if your humidor dips below 62% RH, so watch your readings closely during dry winter months. ## FAQs: Cigar wrapper colors

**Does a darker cigar wrapper mean a stronger cigar?**
Not necessarily. Wrapper color reflects growing and processing conditions, not nicotine content. A dark Maduro wrapper often tastes sweeter and richer, but strength comes from the filler and binder blend. A light Claro cigar can be blended to full strength, and a dark Maduro can be mild.

**What is a "Natural" cigar wrapper?**
Natural is a retail shorthand for medium-brown, sun-grown wrappers, typically Colorado Claro on the Colorado Scale. It means the leaf was grown and fermented without the extended processing that produces a Maduro. Expect balanced, earthy, and slightly creamy flavor notes.

**What is the Colorado Scale for cigars?**
The Colorado Scale is an industry classification system that ranks wrapper leaf color from lightest to darkest across seven categories: Candela, Claro, Colorado Claro, Colorado (Rosado), Colorado Maduro, Maduro, and Oscuro. Color reflects how the leaf was grown, cured, and fermented.

**What is a Candela wrapper and does anyone still make them?**
Candela, also called Double Claro, is a green wrapper produced by quickly heat-curing freshly harvested leaves to lock in chlorophyll. This wrapper was very popular in the U.S. in the 1950s and is sometimes also called American Market Selection, or AMS. A handful of boutique blenders still produce Candela cigars as novelty or nostalgia offerings today.

**What wrapper color is best for a beginner?**
Claro (Connecticut Shade) or Colorado Claro (Natural) wrappers are ideal starting points. Both deliver mild-to-medium body, creamy or balanced flavor profiles, and forgiving smoke that doesn't overwhelm. Colorado Maduro and Maduro are great next steps once you're comfortable with medium-bodied cigars.

**How does wrapper color affect how I should store cigars?**
All premium cigars store best at 65-70% RH and 65-70°F regardless of wrapper shade. Oilier darker wrappers (Maduro, Oscuro) tend to be slightly more resilient to humidity swings, while thin, delicate Claro wrappers can crack more easily if humidity drops below 60% RH.
